# C. Progression and practice structure

Research brief C for the visuospatial trainer: how to make a learner good fast and still get transfer. Compiled 2026-10-30 from full texts or abstracts where reachable (publisher pages, arXiv, author PDFs, journal records). **[unverified]** marks items taken from memory or a secondary source and not checked against the primary source in this session: the web-search quota ran out, and PubMed Central, Sage, PsycNET and Annual Reviews pages could not be read. Numbers are paraphrased; re-check them before quoting in user-facing text.

## 0. Bottom line

- **Difficulty:** keep success near a chance-corrected target p\* = g + (1 − g)·c, where g is the guessing rate and c is success on the above-chance scale (P − g)/(1 − g), default 0.70. That gives 85% on 2-choice items (Wilson et al. 2019's optimum), about 78% on 4-choice and about 70% on open or full-report responses (near Math Garden's 75% and Pelánek's 65%). Higher success buys volume and persistence (Jansen et al. 2013), so offer a "comfortable" preset.
- **Engine:** one logistic ability θ per task family with an uncertainty term: Elo with a decaying K, or a Kalman/Glicko-style update with process noise so that it keeps tracking learning. Generated items are seen once, so difficulty is predicted from generator features (LLTM) and refit on pooled data. A single continuous dimension (MOT speed, exposure time) uses a weighted up-down staircase. Bayesian QUEST+/Psi is for short assessment probes only.
- **Schedule:** a new family starts blocked, with worked examples; interference then rises step by step from blocked to serial to random (Porter & Magill 2010). Incidental features vary from trial 1; structural difficulty dimensions are added one at a time (Raviv et al. 2022).
- **Speed:** train accuracy first, untimed. Deadlines apply only to mastered content, scored with BIS or a rate-correct score against a fixed personal reference.
- **Dose:** spaced sessions of about 20 min, 3–4 a week, plus expanding checks of mastered levels at 3, 7, 14, 30 and 60 days.
- **Transfer:** vary incidentals, interleave item types and similar-looking families (uncued where possible), prompt comparison and bridging, and measure on held-out tasks in a separate no-feedback Benchmark mode. Claim gains on trained tasks; report untrained-task gains as near or mid transfer with a retest caveat; claim nothing far.

## 1. Optimal difficulty

- **Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia & Cohen (2019).** For binary classification learned by stochastic gradient descent with one-trial updates, the error rate that maximises learning speed is 15.87% under Gaussian noise (accuracy ≈ 85%), 18.4% under Laplacian and 25% under Cauchy noise. At a fixed error rate precision grows about as √t; at fixed difficulty it grows much more slowly. The result assumes two categories, a gradient learner, learning that changes only precision, and no batches. The authors set aside multi-category tasks, batch learning and ideal Bayesian learners (for whom trial order is irrelevant), and call the link to flow speculative. The evidence is theory plus simulations (perceptron, MNIST network, the Law & Gold model of monkey perceptual learning), with no human experiment. Use it as a rationale for holding success constant, not as a law for 4-choice or 3D tasks.
- **Math Garden (Klinkenberg, Straatemeier & van der Maas 2011).** Elo-type ratings for children and items are updated after every answer, and items are drawn so that expected success averages .75 (3,648 children, 3.5 M problems in 10 months). Accuracy and time are scored together ("high speed, high stakes"): S = (2x − 1)·a·(d − t) for correctness x, deadline d and time t. Slow answers score near 0 and fast errors cost most; with a 20 s limit, a correct answer at 8 s earns +12 and an error at 5 s earns −15 (Hofman et al. 2018). Children choose a level: the hardest expects about .60 success (Hofman et al. 2018), the easiest about .90 [unverified: secondary source].
- **Jansen et al. (2013).** 207 children (grades 3–6): a control group plus three groups with preset success of about 60, 75 and 90%, for 6 weeks. Maths improved only in the practice groups; higher success led to more problems attempted, and that volume carried the gain. Anxiety fell equally in all groups, control included; perceived competence rose only modestly.
- **Pelánek et al. (2017)**, geography facts, ~37,000 learners, 3.3 M answers: targets of 50–95% yielded achieved success of only ~65–90%; easier targets kept more users in the short term; "appropriate" ratings peaked at 60–70% success; harder practice looked better for learning (attrition bias possible). They propose ~65% (~75% in school). **Papoušek & Pelánek (2017)**, 85,000 learners: letting learners adjust difficulty after each 10-question set gave no overall benefit, and the rating dialog alone reduced engagement.
- **Challenge-skill balance** (flow; Csikszentmihalyi 1990 [unverified]) motivates a mid-range target, but adaptive-practice data show no sharp inverted U (Pelánek et al. 2017).
- **Implication:** the best target depends on the chance rate and on the goal: learning per trial (≈85% on 2-choice items) or volume and persistence (higher). State targets on the c scale and convert with p\* = g + (1 − g)c. This mapping is a design heuristic consistent with these sources, not a tested law.

## 2. Adaptive procedures and moving thresholds

- **Levitt (1971).** A 1-up/k-down rule converges where p = 0.5^(1/k): 50, 70.7, 79.4 and 84.1% for k = 1–4 (k = 4 is close to Wilson's 85%). Start with a large step and shrink it (halve after runs 1, 3, 7 …, or once after run 3); an initial step that is too large costs much less than one that is too small. Run at least 6–8 reversals and estimate from mid-run points (the mean of peaks and valleys) over an even number of runs.
- **Kaernbach (1991), weighted up-down.** Unequal steps make the expected drift zero at any target: Δeasier/Δharder = p/(1 − p), i.e. 3 for 75%, 4 for 80%, 5.7 for 85%. Each change depends only on the last trial. In simulation it was ~5% more precise than 1-up/2-down, best with steps of ~1/6 of the X90–X10 spread (Green et al. 1989, cited in Kaernbach 2001, recommend 1/8–1/4).
- **PEST (Taylor & Creelman 1967).** Stays at a level until a sequential (Wald) test shows performance above or below target; the step halves at reversals and doubles after repeated moves in the same direction [unverified].
- **QUEST (Watson & Pelli 1983).** A Bayesian posterior over threshold, using a fixed-slope Weibull (β = 3.5, lapse δ = .01 for 2AFC). Each trial goes at the posterior mode; the run stops at a trial count or a confidence width. Simulated efficiency after 128 trials was ~84% (PEST ~45%). Assumes a fixed threshold, one function shape across conditions, and independent trials.
- **Psi (Kontsevich & Tyler 1999)** estimates threshold and slope jointly by minimising expected entropy: threshold to ~2 dB in under 30 trials, while the slope needs ~300. **QUEST+ (Watson 2017)** generalises to many stimulus dimensions, parameters and response categories (precomputed likelihood tables, under 1 s per trial in its examples) and explicitly assumes a stationary observer.
- **Moving thresholds.** Bayesian methods weight all past trials equally, so while the person learns the posterior lags and grows overconfident; drift also biases slope estimates, and Doll, Veltink & Buitenweg (2015) found a tracking paradigm most robust. Fixed-step staircases forget old trials and follow drift by design. For training, use a weighted up-down with a small fixed step, or an Elo/Kalman update with process noise (in effect, a Bayesian method with forgetting). Avoid shrinking-step schemes (Levitt halving, PEST) in training. Use QUEST+/Psi with a fresh prior only for short probes where a fixed threshold is plausible.
- **NeuroTracker.** Company pages: ~20 trials per ~6-min session, 8 s of tracking per trial. Speed rises after a fully correct trial and falls after any error (1-up/1-down), so the "speed threshold" is the speed at which ~50% of trials are fully correct; speed 1.0 = 68 cm/s; the baseline is the first 3 sessions. Research protocol (Legault, Allard & Faubert 2013): 1-up/1-down in 0.05 log steps, stopping after 8 reversals, threshold = geometric mean of the last 4. Faubert (2013): 4 targets, up to 15 sessions over ≥ 5 days, at most 3 a day, ~8 min each.

## 3. Item models for generated items

- **Elo (Pelánek 2016).** P = 1/(1 + e^−(θ − b)); after each answer θ += K(x − P) and b −= K(x − P). The K schedule matters: start with K(n) = k₀/(1 + k₁n), k₀ = 1, k₁ = 0.05 (k₀ = 4, k₁ = 0.5 fitted their simulation), then refit by grid search. With guessing, P = g + (1 − g)/(1 + e^−(θ − b)). Elo item difficulties nearly match joint maximum-likelihood estimates but need ~100+ learners per item; Elo tracks changing skill, whereas IRT assumes it fixed. Extensions (multivariate Elo over correlated skills, response times, PFA-style learning terms) add little accuracy.
- **Item selection (Pelánek et al. 2017).** K(n) = 1/(1 + 0.06n). Each candidate is scored as 10 × difficulty match + 120 × (−1/seconds since last seen) + 10 × 1/√(1 + attempts).
- **Uncertainty.** Glicko-2 (Glickman) keeps a rating, a deviation and a volatility; the deviation grows during inactivity. Defaults are 1500/350/0.06 with τ between 0.3 and 1.2, and it works best with 10–15+ games per rating period. For per-trial updates, use a Kalman/Laplace logistic update instead (the same idea, with a rating period of one trial). Urnings (Hofman et al. 2020, on Math Garden data) is an alternative designed to track with quantified uncertainty.
- **LLTM and design.** LLTM (Fischer 1973) constrains Rasch difficulty to a weighted sum of item features, b_i = Σ_k q_ik·η_k + η_0, so a never-seen generated item gets a predicted difficulty; per-item Elo cannot calibrate items that are each seen once. Cognitive design systems (Embretson 1998) derive the generator from a cognitive model of the task and test whether predicted difficulties match observed ones [description unverified]. Vocabulary: radicals change difficulty, incidentals should not (items differing only in incidentals are isomorphs).
- **Spatial evidence.** Rajeb et al. (2025), mental rotation, grades 3–8: six features reproduced Rasch difficulties at r = .90 (~81% of variance). Weights: number of cubes η = 3.65, rotation 1.50, configuration −1.07, occlusion −0.62, mirror vs structural foil −0.59, plus colour. The LLTM still fit worse than the Rasch model, so item-specific residuals remain. Gittler's 3DW (1990) is a Rasch-scaled cube-rotation test. Arendasy & Sommer (2010) and Arendasy, Sommer & Gittler (2010) used automatically generated rotation items to show that specific design features carry sex-related DIF and to split the gender gap into cognitive components. Distractors change the construct: a distractor-free cube-construction format widened item difficulty (p from .02 to .95, against .37 to .63) and raised the correlation with intelligence (.57 against .17) (EJPA study, 146 students).
- **Several difficulty dimensions.** (i) One staircase per dimension, in turn: interpretable but slow, and it ignores interactions. (ii) One θ per family plus LLTM-predicted difficulty: choose any feature vector whose predicted P is near p\*, randomise which dimensions carry the difficulty, and track residuals per dimension. (iii) Multidimensional QUEST+: efficient, but it assumes stationarity and is costly; it suits assessment. **Recommended: (ii)**, with per-dimension residuals (or multivariate Elo) to find weak dimensions.

## 4. Curriculum by learning progress

- **ZPDES and RiARiT (Clement, Roy, Oudeyer & Lopes 2015).** Activities are the arms of a bandit, and the reward is learning progress: the success rate over the last d/2 attempts minus that over the d/2 before. An exploration term γ mixes in uniform choice, and harder activities open when success on easier ones passes a threshold. RiARiT needs an expert activity × knowledge-component matrix; ZPDES needs only a coarse ordering. The paper gives no values for d, γ or the thresholds. In simulation both beat the expert sequence. Field study (400 children aged 7–8, 11 schools, 40 min of exercises): ZPDES reached higher levels on 3 of 4 exercise types, but on the fourth no ZPDES child reached the top level, against 75% with the expert sequence. Pre/post gains were reported only for app vs control, not by algorithm.
- **Randomised trial (Clément, Sauzéon, Roy & Oudeyer, preprint 2024/25; 265 children).** On a 20-item test, ZPDES went from 6.74 to 9.38 and the hand-designed linear sequence from 6.83 to 8.36 (algorithm × time η² = .034). Letting children choose cosmetic features helped under ZPDES and hurt under the linear sequence.
- **Theory (Oudeyer, Gottlieb & Lopes 2016).** Learning progress acts as an intrinsic reward; tasks that are too easy or too hard to predict lose interest.
- **Caveats here.** (1) Target-success selection holds success constant by design, so a progress signal based on success differences collapses; measure progress as change in θ or on fixed probe items. (2) Bandits can starve slow, hard skills, so add coverage constraints. (3) The evidence is children's arithmetic; it is untested for spatial training.

## 5. Mastery, knowledge tracing, guidance

- **Mastery learning.** Bloom (1984): ~1 SD for mastery learning (84th percentile) and ~2 SD for tutoring with mastery (98th); the typical criterion is 80–90% on formative tests [unverified: secondary sources]. The underlying studies were short and used experimenter-made tests [unverified]. Kulik, Kulik & Bangert-Drowns (1990): 108 studies, positive effects, largest for weaker students [unverified: secondary source]; mean ES ~0.5, larger with stricter criteria [unverified].
- **Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (Corbett & Anderson 1995).** A skill is either learned or not, with parameters P(L0), P(T), guess and slip; Cognitive Tutors declare mastery at P(learned) ≥ 0.95. Continuous perceptual skills fit this two-state model poorly.
- **Pelánek & Řihák (2018): simple criteria nearly match model-based ones.** An exponential moving average m ← αm + (1 − α)x declares mastery at m ≥ T, and T = 0.95 is a reasonable compromise. From m = 0, that takes 14 straight correct answers at α = 0.8 and 29 at α = 0.9; N-in-a-row equals α = 0.5 with T = 1 − 0.5^N. BKT with the true parameters barely beat N-in-a-row, and changing the input (e.g., adding response times) mattered more than changing the model. Advice: keep online rules simple and tune thresholds offline from effort-versus-outcome curves.
- **Worked examples and fading (Renkl, Atkinson & Große 2004).** Learners study worked solutions, then solution steps are removed one at a time, with self-explanation prompts. Adaptive fading driven by the tutor's skill estimates beat fixed fading and plain problem solving, in the lab and in classrooms (Salden et al. 2010).
- **Expertise reversal (Kalyuga et al. 2003).** Guidance that helps novices becomes redundant or harmful for more expert learners, so fade it by learner state, not by the calendar.
- **Productive failure (Sinha & Kapur 2021; 53 studies, 166 comparisons).** Problem solving before instruction beat instruction-first on conceptual knowledge and transfer (g = 0.36; 0.37–0.58 for faithful designs) and tied on procedural knowledge (g = −0.03). The evidence covers maths and science concepts, not perceptual-spatial skill. Low priority: at most, offer "try first" on the first 2–3 items of a new family, before the animated explanation.

## 6. Practice schedules

- **Interleaving.** Rohrer & Taylor (2007), volumes of four unusual solids: practice accuracy was 89% blocked vs 60% mixed, but one week later 20% vs 63% (d = 1.34); mixing forces the learner to pick the method. Brunmair & Richter (2019; 59 papers, 238 effects, 8,466 participants): g = 0.42 for inductive category learning; paintings 0.67, photographs 0.35, maths 0.34, expository texts n.s., words −0.39 (blocking better). The benefit grows with between-category similarity and with complexity, shrinks as items within a category become alike, and is larger in younger samples.
- **Contextual interference.** Shea & Morgan (1979), 72 students, three barrier-knockdown patterns: blocked practice was faster during acquisition; random practice retained better when the test context changed and transferred better, most of all to a harder 5-barrier pattern. Brady (2004; 63 studies): mean ES 0.38; lab 0.57, applied 0.19; adults 0.50, high school 0.10, children 0.09. Czyż et al. (2024; 34 studies): delayed-transfer SMD 0.55 (0.40 without outliers), negligible under age 18, larger in the lab; the evidence is of low quality. Porter & Magill (2010): steadily rising interference (blocked → serial → random) beat both pure blocked and pure random practice (retention in golf putting; retention and transfer in basketball passing).
- **Which benefit applies here (inference).** Rohrer-type gains come from discriminating which method a problem needs. When the screen already says which exercise it is, that discrimination is absent, and what remains is contextual interference and spacing, whose applied effects are smaller (Brady: 0.19). So interleave item types inside a family (axes, mirror vs rotated, fold types) and, where the interface allows, mix families uncued so that the learner must choose the transformation.
- **Variability of practice.** Schmidt's (1975) schema theory: varying task parameters builds a rule that generalises to new variants. Raviv, Lupyan & Green (2022): variability slows initial learning but improves generalisation. They distinguish four kinds (numerosity, heterogeneity, situational, scheduling). Novices are hurt by variation on dimensions that decide the answer but helped by variation on irrelevant ones, and "starting small" helps them. Few studies have varied when in training variability is introduced.
- **Consistent vs varied mapping (Schneider & Shiffrin 1977; Shiffrin & Schneider 1977).** A consistent stimulus-response mapping plus long practice yields fast, load-independent automatic detection; with a varied mapping processing stays slow and effortful [details unverified]. New spatial items are mostly varied mapping, so expect gains in strategy and encoding rather than automatic transformation. A finite item space turns into consistent mapping and can be memorised (a cube has only 11 nets), inflating scores without any visualisation; generate from large spaces and ask questions that need the transformation (e.g., where a marked face lands).
- **Desirable difficulties (Bjork & Bjork 2011; Soderstrom & Bjork 2015).** Practice performance is a poor guide to learning. Spacing, interleaving, varied conditions and retrieval lower it now but improve retention and transfer, provided the learner can still succeed. Learners prefer blocked and massed practice because it feels better, so show progress from delayed checks, not from in-session accuracy (which the engine holds constant anyway).
- **Spacing.** Cepeda et al. (2006; 839 assessments, 317 experiments): recall was 47% spaced vs 37% massed; expanding vs equal intervals did not differ significantly (62.0 vs 58.6%). Cepeda et al. (2008; 1,354 people): the optimal gap falls from ~20–40% of a 1-week retention interval to ~5–10% of a 1-year interval, and gaps that are too short cost far more than gaps that are too long (both are fact-learning studies). Donovan & Radosevich (1999; 63 studies): d = 0.46 overall, but 0.97 for simple motor tasks and only 0.07–0.11 for complex ones; 0.71 for rests under 1 min vs 0.16 for rests over 1 day.
- **Dose.** Lampit, Hallock & Valenzuela (2014; 4,885 older adults, 52 data sets): computerised cognitive training g = 0.22. By frequency: 1 session/week 0.34, 2–3/week 0.28, more than 3/week 0.07 (n.s.). Unsupervised home training 0.09 (n.s.) vs supervised groups 0.29. Weak evidence that sessions under 30 min do not work. Molloy et al. (2012), auditory perceptual learning: 100 trials a day (~8 min) matched 200–800 a day with half the total trials, because gains accrue between sessions. Wright et al. (2008): 21 days of 15–20 min (~7 h) halved response time on the trained task. NeuroTracker studies use ~8-min sessions, at most 3 a day.

## 7. Speed and accuracy

- **Combined scores (Liesefeld & Janczyk 2019).** IES = mean correct RT ÷ proportion correct; RCS = correct responses ÷ total RT; LISAS; BIS = z(PC) − z(RT). In diffusion-model simulations only BIS was fairly immune to speed–accuracy trade-offs (spurious effects up to d ≈ 0.12, against ~2 for IES and RCS). BIS is standardised within a data set, so to follow one person over time, standardise against a fixed reference (their first sessions, or community norms per family and level).
- **Deadlines and scoring.** Math Garden's rule (§1) rewards fast correct answers and penalises fast errors, which discourages guessing. Show any such rule to users.
- **When to train speed.** Practice speeds spatial tasks up a lot: in Wright et al. (2008), mental-rotation RT fell from 3,132 to 1,480 ms and the slope from 257 to 140 ms per step, while errors stayed flat. What transferred to the other spatial task was mainly the intercept (encoding and set-up), not the rotation slope. Time limits change what spatial tests measure: they enlarge sex differences in mental rotation (Voyer 2011) [unverified]. So train accuracy untimed, add deadlines only on mastered levels, and keep timed and untimed ratings separate.

## 8. Designing for transfer, and honest claims

- **What is known.** Uttal et al. (2013; 217 studies): spatial training works on average (g = 0.47), lasts, and transfers to untrained spatial tasks. Wright et al. (2008): rotation practice transferred to paper folding and back (mostly in the intercept), more than to a verbal-analogies control task. Simons et al. (2016), on brain training in general: strong evidence for trained tasks, weaker for near transfer, little for far transfer or daily life. Vater, Gray & Holcombe (2021; 29 NeuroTracker studies): none preregistered; only 3 tested far transfer, 2 found nothing and the third trained 7 players; near transfer was mixed. Romeas et al. (2025; 62 academy soccer players): 3D-MOT improved, with no near or far transfer.
- **Learning to learn.** Green & Bavelier (2008) argue that broad transfer is likelier from varied, adaptively difficult, engaging training with informative feedback. Bavelier et al. (2012) add action-game features: fast pace, unpredictability, switching between focused and divided attention, peripheral demands, high load [both unverified]. Zhang et al. (2021; two studies, n = 25 and 52, the second preregistered): 45 h of action games, compared with control games, raised learning rates on a new orientation task and on dual N-back, with no difference in final scores.
- **Hugging and bridging (Perkins & Salomon 1988).** Hugging makes practice resemble the target situation, so transfer happens automatically ("low road"); bridging prompts explicit abstraction and connections ("high road"). **Analogical comparison** (Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson 2003): comparing two cases instead of studying them separately raised transfer from 19% to 48%, and learners rarely compare without a prompt.
- **Taxonomy (Barnett & Ceci 2002).** Classify any transfer claim on nine dimensions, each running from near to far. Content: the learned skill, the performance change measured, the memory demands. Context: knowledge domain, physical, temporal, functional, social, modality [dimension names unverified]. Do not average them into one "transfer" number.
- **Honest-claim ladder for this trainer:**
  - (1) Trained families: measured in Benchmark.
  - (2) Near: untrained variants of a trained family (new objects, axes, response formats).
  - (3) Mid: untrained spatial families (e.g., train rotation, test paper folding).
  - (4) Far (STEM grades, sport, IQ, daily life): no claim. Every claim states its Barnett & Ceci position and allows for retest effects, since one user has no control group; causal claims need the community randomised design in 9e.

## 9. Recommendations

### 9a. Modes and engines

1. **Adaptive** (the default; one family or a mixed "circuit"). Per-family θ updated by a logistic Kalman/Elo rule with process noise; items at p\* from the LLTM; the scheduler of 9d; blocked-to-interleaved stages; worked examples and hints that fade as θ rises.
2. **Levels** (mastery path). Ordered levels, each a region of the feature space, practised blocked. Items are drawn at p\* inside the level's region; once θ outgrows the level, its hardest items come up and success climbs to the gate. The mastery gate of 9b controls progress, new families get animated worked examples, and a failed check reopens its level. Shares θ with Adaptive.
3. **Threshold.** One continuous dimension (MOT speed, exposure time, rotation angle) varies while the others stay fixed. Training uses a weighted up-down at p\*; a "Standard" preset (1-up/1-down, 0.05 log step) gives NeuroTracker-style 50% thresholds.
4. **Speed** (timed). Opens per level after mastery. A deadline staircase sets the time pressure; it keeps its own θ_timed; RCS is shown and BIS used for comparisons.
5. **Free play** (sandbox). The user sets every dimension, with no gates and no targets. Responses still update θ and RT norms. Cosmetic choices (themes, colours) are available in every mode.
6. **Benchmark** (assessment). A fixed protocol: no feedback, standard time limits, Psi/QUEST+ probes with a fresh prior or LLTM-calibrated parallel forms, a frozen item-model version and held-out variants. It keeps its own score series, which training never updates.

| Session | 20 min by default (15–30), in 2–3 blocks with 1-min breaks (~80–200 items depending on the family, or 2–3 MOT blocks of ~6 min). Due checks fill the first 20–30% of items. The evidence conflicts: a weak signal that sessions under 30 min were ineffective in older adults (Lampit) against efficient 8-min sessions in perceptual learning (Molloy). | Molloy 2012; Wright 2008; Faubert 2013; Lampit 2014 |
| Weekly dose | 3–4 sessions on at least 3 days (60–120 min a week), with a soft cap of 45 min a day. Nudge users; never punish broken streaks. | Lampit 2014 (older adults); Donovan & Radosevich 1999 |
| Speed mode | Only on mastered levels. Starting deadline = 1.5 × the untimed median correct RT; shorten by 0.02 log10 after a correct in-time answer, lengthen by 0.02 × p\*/(1 − p\*) after an error or time-out. | Kaernbach 1991; Wright 2008 |
| Speed scoring | Show RCS (correct answers per minute) and points S = (2x − 1)(1 − t/deadline). Compare sessions with BIS against the user's first 3 timed sessions. | Klinkenberg 2011; Liesefeld & Janczyk 2019 |
| Benchmark | Every 2 weeks or 8 sessions, whichever comes later; ~15 min; no feedback. 3–4 families per sitting in rotation (every family recurs within 2–3 sittings), each a 20–30-trial Psi/QUEST+ probe or a 20-item parallel form, plus one held-out variant. | Kontsevich & Tyler 1999; Watson 2017; Barnett & Ceci 2002 |

### 9c. What genuinely conflicts

- **Must be separate modes:**
  - **Mastery gating vs free access.** One restricts which items the learner can reach; the other removes the restriction. Hence Levels vs Free play, with Adaptive in between (no gates, but the engine picks the difficulty).
  - **Training vs measurement.** Training adapts and gives feedback; honest measurement needs a fixed protocol, no feedback and held-out tasks. Benchmark is never fed by the training engine, and the training θ is not a score: it moves with item choice and learning.
  - **Untimed vs timed.** Deadlines change the best strategy and possibly the construct (Voyer 2011 [unverified]). Keep a separate θ_timed, opened per level only after mastery.
  - **Standard 50% threshold vs training at p\*.** Different convergence points give numbers that cannot be compared, so Threshold mode keeps a Standard preset for comparison with the 3D-MOT literature.
- **Settings within one engine.** Blocked vs interleaved is a continuum that moves with the learner (the stages in 9b; Levels is blocked by design; "focus on one family" is a toggle). High vs low success is one parameter c with three presets (learning per trial vs volume and persistence), set once because repeated asking reduced engagement. Worked examples, hints and variability follow learner state (expertise reversal), with a manual override.
- **Shared learner record.** Per family: θ and σ² (untimed and timed) and RT distributions. Per level: EMA, mastery flag, next check date. Per continuous dimension: the threshold. Plus the Benchmark series (read-only to training) and the global LLTM weights.

### 9d. Choosing the next exercise in a mixed session

- **The options:**
  - **(1) Learning-progress bandit.** Puts practice where progress happens and has the best field evidence (ZPDES beat an expert sequence in a randomised trial). But the signal is noisy at our data volumes, collapses under target-success selection unless computed on θ, and can starve slow skills (no ZPDES child reached the top of one exercise type).
  - **(2) Weakest skill.** Easy to explain and right for a stated goal, but it drills plateaued skills, can demotivate, and has no evidence that it maximises learning.
  - **(3) Round-robin with variability constraints.** Guarantees coverage and interleaving and is predictable, but ignores where learning is happening.
- **Recommended: a constrained round-robin skeleton with learning-progress weighting.**
  1. Session plan of three families: a focus family (highest learning progress, or the user's goal), a review family (most overdue check) and a variety family (longest unpractised, or random). MOT counts as one family and runs in blocks.
  2. Next run: softmax over score = LP + 1 if a check is due + (1 − e^(−days since practice/7)). LP = (θ now − θ 60 trials ago) ÷ √(σ²now + σ²then), clipped to ±2; a family with fewer than 60 trials uses the population-average LP for its trial count, which is optimistic for new families, so they get tried (default LP = 1.5 until pooled data exist). With probability ε = 0.15, pick uniformly instead. Run lengths follow the current interleaving stage, and each family gets 20–50% of the session's trials.
  3. Within the family: item at p\* from the LLTM; never one of the last 3 feature combinations; rotate the dimension that carries the difficulty; 10% easy and 10% stretch items.
  4. Weak spots: target the weakest dimension only through the residual flag (a few extra items on it), or when the user picks "Focus on weak spots".

### 9e. Measure and test

- Log every trial: features, predicted P, response, RT, mode, stage and item-model version.
- Judge any scheduler change by delayed Benchmark gains (trained and held-out) and by user retention, never by in-session accuracy. First A/B tests: learning-progress weighting vs plain round-robin; c = 0.70 vs 0.85; staged vs immediate interleaving.
- For community-level causal transfer claims, offer an opt-in randomised crossover: train family A or B for 4 weeks, benchmark both, then swap.
- Audit generators for memorisable item spaces and for distractor patterns that allow elimination strategies.
