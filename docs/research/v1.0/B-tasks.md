# B. Exercise catalogue for a visuospatial trainer

Research brief B for the open-source VSI trainer: which spatial tasks to build, how to generate and verify them, and how difficulty scales. Compiled 2026-10-30. Sources are primary papers where reachable, otherwise reviews, manuals or vendor pages (named as such).
Conventions: **[unverified]** = not checked against a source in this pass (prior knowledge or a secondary page only); **(DJ)** = design judgement, not a research finding; **[gap]** = searched for, not found. The web-search budget ran out late in the pass, so some citation details carry [unverified].
Codes: Uttal et al. (2013) cells: I-S intrinsic-static (example: embedded figures), I-D intrinsic-dynamic (mental rotation), E-S extrinsic-static (water level), E-D extrinsic-dynamic (perspective taking). CHC Gv narrow abilities (McGrew, v2.3): Vz visualization, SR speeded rotation, CF flexibility of closure, CS closure speed, MV visual memory, SS spatial scanning, IM imagery.

## 0. Cross-cutting findings that shape every exercise

- **Malleability and retest.** Uttal et al. (206 studies): mean training effect g = 0.47; transfer within a cell g = 0.51 and across cells g = 0.55; delayed post-tests g = 0.44 vs 0.48 immediately after. Controls improved too (g = 0.45 vs 0.62 for trained groups; 0.75 for controls tested repeatedly), so much of any raw pre–post gain is retest, and an untrained comparison is required.
- **Strategies, not only transformation.** MRT success partly reflects analytic shortcuts (Hegarty 2018). Most Paper Folding solvers use heuristics such as "two holes per fold" rather than mental unfolding (Burte et al. 2018). (DJ) Run cheap adversary solvers (cube counting, hole counting, symmetry, gear parity, the egocentric view) on every generated item pool. They must score near chance, or the exercise trains the shortcut.
- **Solver contract (DJ).** Every generator emits seed, parameters, a stimulus model and a key. An independent solver recomputes the key from the stimulus model and checks four things: (i) exactly one option satisfies the item rule; (ii) every distractor fails by a visible margin; (iii) the deciding features are visible in the rendered image (ID-buffer test); (iv) no degenerate geometry. Continuous-response tasks (MOT, orientation, homing) check that the item is well posed instead.
- **Staircase arithmetic.** 1-up/1-down converges on 50% correct, 2-down/1-up on 70.7%, 3-down/1-up on 79.4% (0.5^(1/n); Levitt 1971). For items with 2–5 options, aim for 70–80% (DJ). Keep 1-up/1-down for MOT so results stay comparable with the literature.
- **Format overlap (DJ).** When an exercise shares its format with a published test (SBST, PFT, SOT, PSVT:R), gains on that test count as near transfer, not mid transfer. Keep the Vandenberg–Kuse MRT format (4 options, 2 correct) out of training so the MRT stays usable as an external check. Several formats also appear in aptitude and admissions tests (CogAT paper folding; DAT Space Relations; the Dental Admission Test's perceptual section with hole punching, cube counting and views) [unverified]; gains on those count as near transfer too.
- **Licensing posture (DJ, not legal advice).** The paradigms can be reimplemented from scratch. Items, figure drawings, layouts, names and score scales from ETS, Pearson, Mind Garden, Purdue, UCSB or Cognisens tests must not be copied.

## 1. 3D multiple-object tracking (3D-MOT)

- **Sources.** MOT itself: Pylyshyn & Storm 1988 [unverified details]. 3D-MOT: Faubert & Sidebottom 2012 (training rationale; standing vs sitting shifts thresholds); Faubert 2013 (n = 308: 102 professional, 173 elite amateur, 33 student); Legault, Allard & Faubert 2013; Plourde et al. 2017. Reviews: Meyerhoff et al. 2017; Vater et al. 2021; Holcombe 2023.
- **Measures.** Not among the CHC Gv narrow abilities (McGrew v2.3). In a factor analysis of 636 people, MOT loaded on an "attentional capacity" factor together with visual working memory, spatial span and digit-symbol coding (Treviño et al. 2021). That makes it Gwm attentional control/capacity with Gv support (DJ). It falls outside Uttal's typology; the nearest cell is E-D (DJ).
- **Core trial.** 8 identical spheres in a virtual cube. 4 targets are cued (1 s in Faubert 2013, 2 s in later reports), then all spheres move for 8 s on straight paths, bouncing off the walls and each other. The observer then identifies all the targets (verbally in the lab), and a trial counts as correct only if every target is found. Speed follows a 1-up/1-down rule on that criterion, converging on the speed at which 50% of trials are fully correct.
- **Lab staircase.** Steps of 0.05 log10 units (×1.122 per trial). Threshold = (geometric) mean of the last 4 reversals. The run stops after 8 reversals (Legault 2013: 9 spheres, 3–4 targets, 10 s, start 3.75 cm/s, stereo CAVE with a 42° front face) or 20 reversals (Plourde 2017: 8 spheres, 3 targets, 6 s, start 68 cm/s, anaglyph tablet at 40 cm). Romeas et al. 2025 (custom Unity HMD app): start 68 cm/s, 20 trials, mean of the last 4 reversals.
- **Commercial CORE (vendor docs and reviews; product claims).** 20 trials per session (about 6–8 min). The step shrinks during a session (size unpublished). Scores are normalised to 4 targets. Speed 1.0 = 68 cm/s when the viewing distance equals the display diagonal. Baseline = mean of the first 3 CORE sessions. 15–30 sessions are used to judge learning rate.
- **Dose in studies.** 10 sessions in Parsons et al. 2016 (3×20 trials each), Romeas et al. 2016 and Vartanian et al. 2016; up to 15 in Faubert 2013; 3–5 sessions of 80 trials in Harris et al. 2020a; 20 in Scharfen & Memmert 2021; at least 16 single-task plus 10 dual-task in Romeas et al. 2025; up to 76 sessions of 10–90 min across the literature (Vater 2021).
- **Does stereo matter?** Faubert lists binocular disparity as a core feature, but the core papers do not test it. In a within-subject test (anaglyph, n = 60), stereo raised thresholds in adults (p = .001) and children (p = .008) but not in older adults (Plourde 2017). Splitting objects across two disparity planes improved tracking (Viswanathan & Mingolla 1999, n = 5). A 12.3-inch tablet with anaglyph glasses trained as well as a 3D projector with shutter glasses (d = 1.91 vs 1.65; Harris 2020a), but both were stereo. Labs also run flat 2D versions with the same staircase (Zwierko et al. 2022: 8 balls, 3 targets, 10 s, 0.05 log steps). → Stereo raises thresholds in adults and children, not older adults. Whether stereo is needed for training gains has not been tested [gap]. Thresholds from different display modes are not comparable.
- **Theory for design.** Pylyshyn's FINST account proposed a fixed set of discrete pointers; later data argue against a hard cap (Holcombe 2023). The speed limit falls steadily with each added target, from about 8 trackable objects at slow speeds to 1 at the fastest, with no break at 4. That argues for a flexible resource rather than fixed slots. Crowding hurts more at high speed (Alvarez & Franconeri 2007).
- **Theory for design (cont.).** Errors track close target–distractor encounters (cumulative distance); speed acts mostly by producing more of them, with a smaller direct speed cost remaining (Franconeri et al. 2010). The left and right hemifields track largely independently (Alvarez & Cavanagh 2005, via Holcombe 2023). Identity tracking (MIT) is weaker than position tracking: 1.4–2.6 vs 2.3–3.4 items. Making objects look unique helps position tracking by about 0.6 items (Horowitz et al. 2007). Repeated trajectories improve scores (Meyerhoff 2017), and looking at the target centroid predicts success (Harris et al. 2020b).
- **Scoring.** Log speed threshold, plus targets correct per trial. For fixed-speed probes, use guessing-corrected capacity (Hulleman 2005; Alvarez & Franconeri 2007). Marking all targets gives more measurement per trial; probing a single object keeps chance constant across set sizes (Meyerhoff 2017).
- **Correlates.** Corsi span uniquely predicts MOT (sr = .42, n = 92), visuospatial tests together give R² = .30, and nonspatial executive tests correlate only about .1–.2 (Trick et al. 2012). MOT correlates with fluid reasoning (WASI-II), most strongly at a 4-target load (Tullo et al. 2018; similar in a poster by Perelmiter et al. 2024). No direct MOT correlation with MRT or PFT was found [gap]. Reliability: an open 15-min PsychoPy test has test–retest r = .87 (8 discs, 4 targets, 8°/s, 8 s, 50 trials; Meyerhoff & Papenmeier 2020); internal consistency .92 (Treviño 2021).
- **Trainability and transfer.** Gains on the trained task are large and consistent: d = 1.6–2.0 vs a non-significant d = 0.45 in controls (Harris 2020a). Vater et al. (2021) reviewed 29 studies; of 16 interventions, 10 meet minimum criteria (non-NeuroTracker control plus a transfer test) and none were preregistered. Evidence for WM and attention transfer is very weak, and 2 of 3 field-transfer tests were null.
- **Trainability and transfer (cont.).** Romeas 2016 found better soccer passing decisions with groups of 9/7/7 players; a larger replication (Romeas et al. 2025, n = 62, single- then dual-task training, HMD) found no near transfer (IVA+Plus) and no far transfer (game performance). Harris 2020a found no transfer to 2D MOT beyond retest, and an n-back gain the authors say may be inflated by groups unmatched at baseline. Scharfen & Memmert 2021 found narrow transfer only. WM-span gains (Vartanian 2016, n = 41, with active and passive controls) and cognitive-battery gains (Parsons 2016, n = 20, passive control, two authors tied to the vendor) come from small, unreplicated studies. Legault & Faubert 2012 reported transfer to biological-motion perception [unverified].
- **Pitfalls and solver.** Reject end states where a target's and a distractor's projected disks overlap or are too close to tap separately, or where a target is occluded. After the cue, targets must look identical to distractors (no role-linked shading or size cues). Log close encounters per trial and keep their distribution stable across levels (DJ). Use fixed-timestep, seeded physics so trials replay exactly, and never reuse a trajectory for the same user. Visual angle depends on viewing distance: a phone in landscape at about 35 cm spans roughly 23°×11°, versus 35–46° volumes in the lab, so norms must be device-specific (DJ).
- **Open vs proprietary.** Open: the Meyerhoff & Papenmeier PsychoPy test on OSF (free to use; no license stated). Proprietary: NeuroTracker (trademark; vendor score scale) and US 10,706,730 B2 (inventors Faubert & Castonguay; assignee Cognisens). That patent claims a stereoscopic 3D-MOT trainer whose controller runs a core set, a low-motor-load set and a higher-motor-load set, with subject speed commands. Avoid the name, the units and normalisation, and the core-to-motor-load sequence. The generic paradigm dates from 1988; get a legal check.

## 2. Mental rotation

- **Sources.** Shepard & Metzler 1971: RT rises linearly with angle, with similar slopes for picture-plane and depth rotation; the rate is often quoted as about 60°/s [unverified]. Vandenberg & Kuse 1978 (MRT); Peters et al. 1995 (redrawn MRT); Ganis & Kievit 2015 (open library); reviews by Jost & Jansen 2023 and Hegarty 2018.
- **Measures.** Vz for 3D polycubes; SR for speeded rotation of simple 2D figures. Uttal I-D (the MRT is Uttal's example for the cell).
- **Formats.** Chronometric: two views, same vs mirror; scored by RT slope (ms/deg), intercept and accuracy. Psychometric (V&K): a criterion figure and 4 alternatives, 2 of them correct; 2×10 items at 3 min each; an item scores only if both correct choices are marked, so chance is about 3.3/20 (Hegarty 2018). Redrawn MRT: 24 items in four versions: A basic, B alternate, C two-axis rotations (very hard), D horizontal axis only (SILC page).
- **Difficulty used in research.** Angle 0–180° (20° steps in Shepard–Metzler-style sets, e.g. Khooshabeh et al. 2013; 0/50/100/150° in G&K). Axis: the vertical axis is usually fastest (as noted by G&K); two-axis rotation is hardest (MRT-C). Size: 7–11 cubes in 4 arms (G&K); 10 cubes (SILC library; the Shepard–Metzler originals too [unverified]). Foil type: structural foils can be rejected without rotating; mirror foils require rotation (Hegarty 2018). The number of alternatives (2 vs 8) and time limits change scores and sex gaps (Jost & Jansen 2023; Voyer 2011).
- **Norms.** G&K validation (n = 54): mean RT rose from 1.80 s at 0° to 3.19 s at 150°, about 9.6 ms/deg (~100°/s); error rose from 5.3% to 18.8%.
- **Strategies.** Checking global shape (are the end arms parallel or perpendicular?) predicts accuracy; counting cubes predicts errors (r = −.48) (Hegarty 2018). Teaching the global-shape strategy improved MRT scores and removed the sex gap (Boone & Hegarty 2017, as cited by Hegarty 2018). Good imagers rotate more holistically and poor imagers piecemeal; fragmented figures push good imagers into piecemeal processing (Khooshabeh et al. 2013).
- **Other stimulus classes for transfer.** Letters and random polygons (Cooper & Shepard 1973; Cooper 1975, via the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy [unverified details]); hands and feet (Parsons 1987) [unverified]; human bodies and everyday objects (Jost & Jansen 2023); molecules in chemistry (Stieff 2007) [unverified].
- **Training and transfer.** Wright et al. 2008 (n = 31): 21 days × 114 trials online. Mental-rotation and paper-folding practice transferred to each other more than to a verbal-analogies control, and held for novel items, so the gain was process-based. It appeared mostly in intercepts, and the initial sex gap largely closed. Meneghetti et al. 2016 (women only, 16 per group; active and passive controls): MR training transferred to object rotation and perspective taking, held at 1 month, and did not transfer to visual or verbal tasks. Tetris skill stayed specific to Tetris-like shapes (Sims & Mayer 2002). Terlecki et al. 2008 reported durable, general MR gains from long video-game practice [unverified].
- **Pitfalls and solver (1–3).** (1) Chirality: compute canonical keys under the 24 proper rotations. If the mirror's key is in that set, the shape is achiral and its "mirror" trial is really a "same" trial. (2) Rotational symmetry (e.g., C2) shrinks the effective angle, so take the minimum angle over the shape's symmetry group. (3) Occlusion and foreshortening: render an ID buffer and require every arm, especially the end arms that carry chirality, to be visible above a pixel threshold and not aligned with the line of sight. G&K picked their angles to avoid self-occlusion.
- **Pitfalls and solver (4–5).** (4) A supposed "different" item can be solvable as "same" about another axis; this happened with G&K stimuli 29 and 48. The solver must therefore search all rotations, not only the generating axis. (5) Foils need the same cube count. Use shading (as G&K did) and perspective projection to avoid depth ambiguity.
- **Open vs proprietary.** G&K 2015: 384 images (48 objects × 4 angles × same/different), on figshare under CC BY 4.0. Usable with attribution, best as a validation set for our generator's RT slope (DJ). The SILC Shepard–Metzler library (16 objects × 73 views, plus mirrors) is free but may not be shared or posted, so it is unusable in an open project. The redrawn MRT is copyrighted and restricted to faculty and graduate students.

## 3. Paper folding, surface development and cube nets, cube comparisons

- **Sources.** Ekstrom et al. 1976, the ETS Kit: VZ-1 Form Board, VZ-2 Paper Folding, VZ-3 Surface Development, S-1 Card Rotations, S-2 Cube Comparisons. Shepard & Feng 1972 (folding cube nets mentally). Burte et al. 2018 (Paper Folding item attributes and strategies); Burte et al. 2019 (Make-A-Dice).
- **Measures.** Paper folding, nets and surface development: Vz, I-D. Cube comparisons: the ETS spatial-orientation factor, which I map to CHC SR (DJ); I-D.
- **Formats.** VZ-2: 20 items; a square sheet is folded 1–3 times and punched once, and the solver picks the unfolded sheet from 5 options [time limit of 2×3 min unverified]. VZ-3: match numbered net edges to lettered edges of a drawn solid [details unverified]. S-2: two drawn cubes with a different symbol on each face; judge "can be the same cube" vs "different"; 21 items and 3 min per part (Falomir 2026), 42 items in all (Huang et al. 2025). S-1 Card Rotations is the 2D analogue (rotated vs mirrored flat figures) [format unverified]. Shepard & Feng: a net with two marked edges; do they meet once folded? Make-A-Dice: fill a net with 1–6 so that opposite faces sum to 7.
- **Difficulty.** Paper folding: accuracy falls as folds increase, and the number of folds relevant to the answer predicts it better than the total. Fold types are horizontal, vertical, corner and diagonal; diagonal folds are harder, and diagonal mixed with horizontal or vertical is hardest. Occlusion had no effect, though few items had any (Burte 2018). Generators have gone up to 5 folds (Huang et al. 2025). Nets: RT rises with the total number of squares carried during folding, across ten problem classes (Shepard & Feng 1972, via Lovett & Forbus 2013); nets with long rows allow shortcuts (Burte 2019).
- **Scoring.** Accuracy, which ETS corrects for guessing [unverified formula]; RT in chronometric versions. Make-A-Dice: α = .88–.95, test–retest .55–.75.
- **Strategies and pitfalls.** In Paper Folding, a "count folds × 2 holes" heuristic fit behaviour best; only about 22% of solvers mainly unfolded (Burte 2018). Distractors must therefore have the correct hole count with wrong positions, plus "missed the last fold" and "wrong axis" foils. Solver: track layers through exact reflections; reject punches within a margin of a fold line or edge, and holes that merge after unfolding.
- **Pitfalls (cont.).** Nets: when orientation matters, use glyphs with no rotational symmetry (avoid O, X, +, H, I, N, S, Z…); chiral glyphs (F, J, L, P, R) also expose mirror errors (DJ). Enumerate the 24 cube rotations to prove exactly one option fits. Cube comparisons: if two cubes share no visible symbol, they can always be the same (seen from opposite corners), so require at least one shared face (DJ). A "different" item must be impossible under every rotation and every assignment of the hidden faces.
- **Training and transfer.** Wright 2008 (see §2). The think3d! origami and pop-up curriculum (at least 6 × 45 min) raised Paper Folding from 42% to 50% and Purdue rotations from 29% to 35%, with no control group (Burte et al. 2017).
- **Open vs proprietary.** ETS Kit items are licensed research instruments (royalty per copy, research use only, item-security conditions): do not copy items or test names (VZ-2 etc.). Huang et al. 2025 generated Paper Folding and Cube Comparisons items procedurally (random fold axes followed by reverse unfolding; cubes encoded as top/front/right faces plus orientation) but digitised, rather than generated, Surface Development. The Make-A-Dice forms are printed in the paper's appendices (no license stated).

## 4. Cross-sections

- **Sources.** Santa Barbara Solids Test (SBST; Cohen & Hegarty 2007, 2012); Mental Cutting Test (MCT; CEEB 1939; Tsutsumi et al. 2005, 2013).
- **Measures.** Vz; I-D (DJ: it transforms a single object).
- **Formats.** SBST: 30 items; a solid with a cutting plane; choose the cross-section, seen head-on, from 4 options. MCT: 25 items, 5 options, 20 min.
- **Difficulty.** SBST crosses structure (simple, joined, embedded) with plane (orthogonal or oblique, 15 items each). Mean accuracy was .54, and oblique items were harder at every level. Embedded-oblique items were hardest (.36) and embedded-orthogonal easiest (.75); joined items were not harder than simple ones (Cohen & Hegarty 2007). The MCT has "pattern" problems (shape only) and "quantity" problems, where proportions or angles also matter (Tsutsumi 2013).
- **Scoring and diagnostics.** Accuracy. SBST distractors diagnose errors: egocentric (the shape seen without taking the plane's viewpoint; the most chosen, .23), combination (a hybrid of two sections) and alternate (another valid slice). α = .86; r = .50 with a composite of the MRT and a views test, .56 for oblique items.
- **MCT errors.** Building the section only from visible contour intersections; mislocating the plane; answers that look like projections; overestimating lengths; stereotyping (cube → square, cylinder → circle) (Tsutsumi 2013).
- **Training and transfer.** MCT scores rose after descriptive-geometry courses (e.g., from 15.4 to 18.8 out of 25) while a reference group stayed flat; men scored higher in most groups (Tsutsumi 2005). MCT also rose after Sorby's course (Sorby 2005). Cohen & Hegarty 2014 trained cross-section inference with interactive animations [unverified].
- **Pitfalls and solver.** Clip planes against polyhedra exactly; use analytic conics for cylinders and cones. Reject tangent planes and planes near a vertex, where a tiny shift changes the polygon type. Draw the inner solid of embedded items translucent. Check that the egocentric foil differs from the answer, since the two coincide when the plane is parallel to the view. Keep true scale in the options, or proportion errors become undetectable. Require a minimum shape distance between every pair of options.
- **Open vs proprietary.** SBST: no license stated, and the 29-item Inquisit version is a copyrighted script, so do not copy its figures. MCT item set: copyright status unclear, so do not copy.

## 5. Perspective taking and spatial orientation

- **Sources.** Kozhevnikov & Hegarty 2001; Hegarty & Waller 2004 (PTSOT); Friedman et al. 2020 (revised, computerised SOT); He, Chrastil & Hegarty 2022 (a VR walking task and a 32-item SOT).
- **Measures.** Spatial orientation, i.e. perspective taking. It is separable from mental rotation but highly correlated with it (Hegarty & Waller 2004). There is no dedicated CHC narrow code (DJ). Uttal E-D.
- **Format.** A top-down array of 7 objects. Each item reads "imagine standing at A, facing B; point to C", answered by drawing a line on an arrow circle whose top is the facing direction. 12 items in 5 min. The revision uses non-directional objects, dots at object centres and practice with feedback, and the computer version scores itself.
- **Difficulty.** Imagined perspective shift of 0–180°; error grows with the shift (r = .50; He 2022). Beyond about 90°, characteristic errors suggest people fall back on coding the array relative to their own body (Kozhevnikov & Hegarty 2001). Targets that lie on the front/back or left/right axis are easier than oblique ones.
- **Scoring.** Mean absolute angular error (0–180°; chance = 90°, which is also the score given to unanswered paper items). Paper vs computer r = .68–.71; men about 9° lower error; correlations with the Money Road Map and SBSOD of −.31 to −.36 (Friedman 2020). The 32-item SOT has split-half reliability .80 and correlates r = .69 (.86 disattenuated) with the VR walking task (He 2022).
- **3D and map variants.** "Which picture shows the view from X" tasks: Piaget's three mountains (Uttal's E-D example), and the Visualization of Views test used by Cohen & Hegarty [format unverified]. A misaligned map, one not turned to match the view, carries a cost (Levine et al. 1982) [unverified].
- **Training and transfer.** MR training transferred to perspective taking (Meneghetti 2016). No trials that trained perspective taking directly were found in this pass [gap].
- **Pitfalls and solver.** Directional or animate objects bias answers, which is why Friedman replaced them. Avoid near-collinear stand/face/target triples, and facing objects too close to the standing point. On a phone, users can rotate the device, the modern version of turning the booklet, so lock orientation and void any trial with a device-rotation event (DJ). Handle the 0°/360° wrap-around when scoring, and log errors above 90° as front/back confusions.
- **Open vs proprietary.** The PTSOT packet is © UC Regents. Friedman's Java program and stimuli are on OSF with no license stated; He 2022's data are on GitHub with no license file. Reimplement with our own arrays.

## 6. Orthographic and isometric projection, block structures

- **Sources.** PSVT:R (Guay 1976/1977); Revised PSVT:R (Yoon 2011; Maeda & Yoon 2011); Sorby's spatial-skills course (Sorby 2005, 2009, 2010); block counting (Kurtuluş & Yolcu 2013).
- **What PSVT:R is.** A rotation test drawn in isometric projection: apply the rotation shown on an example object to a new object [analogy format from prior knowledge, unverified]. 30 items, 5 options, 20 min, scored as number correct; 13 symmetric and 17 asymmetric isometric objects with inclined, oblique and curved surfaces. Vz, I-D. Matching views and counting blocks are Vz, I-D/I-S (DJ).
- **Psychometrics.** The original had 10 figural errors across 7 items (Yue, cited by Maeda & Yoon). The revision rescaled the figures and ordered items by IRT difficulty; α ≈ .84, one factor, fairly easy for first-year engineering students.
- **Sorby course.** Modules: isometric sketching, orthographic projection, 2D→3D, scaling, rotations, reflections, 3D coordinates, translations, cross-sections, solids of revolution, combining objects. PSVT:R rose from 51.7% to 82.0%. Women's retention in engineering was 76.7% vs 47.8% in a comparison group (2010 slides). A 1-credit version raised PSVT:R from 48.3 to 73.7 and the MCT by 17.8 [units as reported] (Sorby 2005). The comparison groups were self-selected, so causal claims are weak, as Sorby notes.
- **Block counting.** Hidden cubes and faces cause most errors; children's strategies include counting layers and using symmetry. The study did not test any training (Kurtuluş & Yolcu 2013).
- **Pitfalls and solver.** Three views rarely pin down a voxel structure, so a multiple-choice item must have exactly one option whose rendered views match; compare line drawings that include depth-step edges. "Count the cubes" is ambiguous when columns are hidden, so ask for the minimum or maximum count consistent with the views and rules, and compute both exactly (DJ). Rotation-analogy items with symmetric objects can have two right answers, so check the options against the object's symmetry group.
- **Open vs proprietary.** PSVT:R (Purdue Research Foundation) and its revision (available on request): do not copy. Sorby's materials are commercial books and software [unverified]; reuse only the module ideas.

## 7. Visuospatial working memory

- **Sources.** Corsi 1972; Kessels et al. 2000 (standardisation); Berch et al. 1998 (method review) [unverified details]; De Lillo et al. 2016 (sequence structure).
- **Measures.** Gwm (spatial WM capacity) plus Gv-MV. Outside Uttal's typology; nearest cells are E-S for static locations and E-D with updating (DJ).
- **Format.** 9 blocks in an irregular layout, tapped at 1 block/s. Start at length 2 with two trials per length; move up if either trial is correct and stop when both fail. Scores: span, total correct, and their product (Kessels 2000). Backward recall is the usual harder variant.
- **Difficulty.** Length 2–9. Path crossings lower recall, spatial structure and grouping help, and path length matters little (De Lillo 2016). 3D and dynamic variants rotate the board between encoding and recall (spatial updating) or use a walking, room-scale layout [unverified].
- **Training and transfer.** WM training gives near transfer to other WM tests but no convincing far transfer (Melby-Lervåg et al. 2016). Corsi span is the best single predictor of MOT (Trick 2012).
- **Pitfalls and solver.** Keep minimum spacing between blocks and avoid accidental groupings unless intended. Set crossings and structure explicitly in the generator. Time presentation with requestAnimationFrame, and give blocks generous touch areas.
- **Open vs proprietary.** The paradigm is unowned; Inquisit and Vienna Test System versions are commercial implementations. Caution: Wechsler-family batteries have used a Corsi-type board (Spatial Span, e.g. WMS-III [unverified]; Parsons 2016 used Spatial Span as an outcome). Use our own layouts, never the Wechsler board, and tell users that practice may raise scores on clinical span tests.

## 8. Disembedding, closure and spatial scanning

- **Sources.** Witkin's EFT and GEFT (Oltman, Raskin & Witkin 1971). ETS Kit: CF-1 Hidden Figures, CF-2 Hidden Patterns, CS-1 Gestalt Completion, CS-3 Snowy Pictures, SS-1 Maze Tracing, SS-2 Choosing a Path, SS-3 Map Planning.
- **Measures.** CF (disembedding), CS (closure speed), SS (scanning). Embedded figures are Uttal's I-S example.
- **Formats.** GEFT: find a simple shape inside a complex one; 25 items in 20 min. CF-1: say which of 5 simple figures is embedded in a pattern, at the same size and orientation [unverified]. CS-1: name the object in a fragmented picture. Scoring: number correct within the time limit; RT per item when untimed.
- **Difficulty.** Density of the complex pattern; camouflage from lines that continue through the target; number and similarity of candidate figures; target size. For closure: fragmentation or noise level (Huang et al. 2025 used noise severity as a parameter).
- **Training and transfer.** A meta-analysis of training in young children found I-S gains of g = 0.46 (Yang et al. 2020). No evidence on training adults' disembedding was found in this pass [gap].
- **Pitfalls and solver.** Dense patterns embed distractor candidates by accident, so run an exhaustive translation-only subgraph search on a lattice. Never let one candidate contain another. Decide, and teach, whether a figure edge may lie on a longer line. Closure needs recognisable objects, which means licensed or self-made image sets. Mazes: generate perfect mazes with BFS-unique solutions and no visually ambiguous crossings.
- **Open vs proprietary.** GEFT is copyrighted (Mind Garden sells print permission per booklet), and ETS items are licensed. Generate lattice figures instead, as Huang et al. 2025 did.

## 9. Mental animation and mechanical reasoning

- **Sources.** Hegarty 1992 (pulleys) [unverified details; findings via Hegarty 2004]; Hegarty & Sims 1994; Sims & Hegarty 1997; Schwartz & Black 1996 (gears) [unverified citation; findings via Hegarty 2004]; Hegarty 2004 (review).
- **Measures.** Vz applied to causal chains, plus mechanical knowledge; I-D (DJ).
- **Format.** A static diagram with a statement to verify (e.g., "pulley C turns clockwise": true or false), or an arrow showing a direction to verify. Scored by accuracy and RT.
- **Difficulty.** Where the queried part sits in the causal chain: errors and RT rise further down the chain, and eye fixations go to earlier parts (Hegarty 1992, via Hegarty 2004). With gears, judgement time scales with rotation angle until people work out the parity rule (neighbouring gears turn opposite ways) and switch to it (Schwartz & Black).
- **Correlates.** Errors correlate with Paper Folding r = −.70, the Vandenberg MRT −.50, spatial orientation −.49 and the Bennett mechanical test −.49, and spatial ability predicts beyond verbal ability (Hegarty & Sims 1994). A visuospatial memory load interferes more than a verbal one (Sims & Hegarty 1997).
- **Training.** Effects of training mental animation in general are not established [gap].
- **Pitfalls and solver.** Linear gear chains reduce to the parity rule, so add branches, belts (open belts keep direction, crossed belts reverse it), odd loops that jam, and pulleys (DJ). Solver: two-colour the gear graph (an odd cycle means a jam) and use teeth ratios for speed. Leave clear gaps, at least 15% of the radius, between gears that do not mesh (DJ).
- **Open vs proprietary.** The Bennett Mechanical Comprehension Test is commercial [unverified owner]: do not copy its items.

## 10. Large-scale navigation

- **Sources.** Triangle completion (Loomis et al. 1993) [unverified]; Virtual SILCton (Weisberg et al. 2014) [unverified]; the Santa Barbara Sense of Direction scale, SBSOD (Hegarty et al. 2002) [unverified citation]; Sea Hero Quest (Coutrot et al. 2022); He et al. 2022.
- **Measures.** Environmental spatial ability: E-D at large scale. Uttal's typology centres on small-scale tasks (DJ).
- **Formats.** Triangle completion: travel two legs, then turn and return to the start; scored by absolute angular and distance error. SILCton: learn routes through a virtual campus, then point between buildings and build a map [unverified details]. SBSOD: a short self-report scale [unverified item count] that correlates −.33 to −.36 with SOT error (Friedman 2020).
- **What works in a browser (DJ).** SBSOD: trivially, but it is a covariate, not something to train. Desktop first-person route learning plus pointing: feasible in WebGL. Visual-only triangle completion: feasible on desktop and phone, but it measures visual path integration. Body-based integration needs room-scale WebXR; walking added about 5.5° of error, and distance error behaved as a separate ability (He 2022). Sea Hero Quest shows mobile navigation tasks can reach millions of players (Coutrot 2022). On phones, motion sickness and control skill confound the scores.
- **Trainability.** Navigation training and transfer were not reviewed in this pass [gap].
- **Open vs proprietary.** SILCton and Sea Hero Quest are research software with their own terms [unverified]: reimplement rather than reuse.

## 11. Imagery and mental synthesis

- **Sources.** Finke & Slayton 1988 (mentally combining simple parts into recognisable patterns) [unverified]; overlay/superimposition tasks [gap: no source checked]; form boards (ETS VZ-1); Tetris (Sims & Mayer 2002). Measures IM and Vz; I-D (DJ).
- **Assessment (DJ).** Finke-style synthesis is scored by judges, so a solver cannot verify it. Overlay tasks ("what do A and B make when superimposed?") are easy to verify with pixel logic but untested for transfer. "Will it fit" packing can be verified by exact cover, but Tetris skill stayed shape-specific, and 2D piece assembly resembles Wechsler Visual Puzzles (§13). Defer all three. No open item sets or transfer evidence were found [gap].

## 12. Water-level task

- **Sources.** Piaget & Inhelder 1948/1956; Vasta & Liben 1996 (review). Measures horizontality in a tilted frame; Uttal's E-S example; no clear CHC code (DJ). The paradigm is unowned.
- **Facts.** Many adults fail: in one study 32% of college women and 15% of men, d ≈ 0.44–0.66 (secondary source: Wikipedia). Training raises scores but did not erase the sex gap (Vasta, Knott & Gaze 1996) [unverified]. Experienced bartenders and servers did no better (Hecht & Proffitt 1995) [unverified].
- **Use (DJ).** Cheap, with a continuous angular-error score, but learning the rule removes much of the error at once. Poor as an exercise; useful as an untrained E-S probe.

## 13. Block Design and Visual Puzzles (do not clone)

- **What they are.** Kohs (1920) had people arrange coloured cubes to copy designs; Hutt (1932) added timing, and Wechsler adopted the task in 1939. WISC-V (2014): Visual Spatial Index = Block Design + Visual Puzzles. WAIS-5 (2024): Visual Spatial = the same two subtests; in WAIS-IV both sat in Perceptual Reasoning. Block Design: copy a model with red-and-white blocks, timed, with bonus points on harder items. Visual Puzzles: pick the 3 pieces that make a target figure [time limit unverified]. Both are mainly Vz; Uttal I-D/I-S (DJ).
- **Contamination risk.** Practising these formats inflates later clinical scores through practice effects, which distorts diagnoses and norms. Copying items would breach Pearson's copyright, close format clones undermine test security, and either would make our own transfer claims uninterpretable (teaching to the test). Exclude both tasks and their near-clones: two-colour split-face cube patterns and choose-k-pieces assembly (DJ). Never label one of our scores "VSI", which would imply equivalence to the Wechsler index (DJ).

### (b) Held-out probes (never trained, not even as warm-ups; distinct look and UI)

| Probe | Construct | Format (one form) | Mid transfer from |

| P5 **Level** (optional) | horizontality · E-S | 12 items: water lines and plumb lines in tilted frames; mean absolute angular error | across-cell check |

- **Alternate forms.** At least 3 forms per probe from one blueprint: the same parameter grid, only seeds differ. Pilot-equate the forms, targeting reliability ≥ .80, then counterbalance them across users. Probes give no feedback, do not adapt, and each take at most about 6 min. Give them at baseline, after about 10 sessions, at the end, and at a 4–8 week follow-up.
- **Discriminant control.** Add one non-spatial control probe, e.g. verbal analogies as in Wright 2008, to estimate non-specific gains such as motivation and familiarity with testing.
- **Evaluation design (DJ).** Randomise users to cluster A (Fold, Nets, Spin, Rotate, Slice) or cluster B (Orient, Views, Span, Track, Hidden) for the first 4–6 weeks. P1 and P3 are the targets for A; P2 and P4 for B. Each cluster serves as the other's retest control, after which users cross over. Preregister the analysis (none of the NeuroTracker studies were preregistered; Vater 2021) and report effect sizes with confidence intervals.

### (c) Difficulty dimensions to adapt

| Exercise | Dimensions and ranges | Rule | Primary score |
|---|---|---|---|
| Track | Speed 1–40°/s, start ~5°/s (DJ; typical fixed speeds 1–15°/s, Meyerhoff 2017; self-set limits 0–42°/s, Alvarez & Franconeri 2007); targets 2–5 among 8–10 (standard 4 of 8); duration 5–15 s (standard 8 s); minimum spacing held fixed within a level (spacing drives errors) | 1-up/1-down on all-correct, 0.05 log10 steps, 20 trials | Geometric mean of last reversals, or a logistic fit |
| Rotate | Angle 0–180° in 30° bins (check every rendered view for self-occlusion, as G&K did); axis vertical → horizontal → picture plane → oblique; 6–12 cubes; mirror foils (structural foils only in a strategy tutorial) | 3-down/1-up on level; angles mixed | Accuracy; RT slope and intercept |
| Spin | Picture-plane angle 0–180°; polygon vertices 6–14; deadline from 3.0 s down to 0.8 s | 2-down/1-up on deadline | Correct per minute; RT slope |
| Fold | Folds 1–5; fold type H/V → corner → diagonal → mixed; punches 1–3; heuristic-proof foils from level 2 | 3-down/1-up | Accuracy |
| Nets | Total squares carried from about 1–2 up to ~10 (DJ; original ranges unverified); long-row → short-row nets; question type: opposite face → edges meet → which cube (plain colours; oriented glyphs are reserved for probe P1) | 3-down/1-up | Accuracy, RT |
| Slice | Plane orthogonal → single oblique → compound oblique; structure simple → joined → embedded; polyhedra → curved solids; option shape-distance margin shrinking | 3-down/1-up | Accuracy; egocentric-error rate |
| Views | 4–14 cubes; height 1–4; direction views → isometric or isometric → views; 0–3 hidden cubes | 3-down/1-up | Accuracy |
| Orient | Maximum perspective shift 45 → 90 → 135 → 180°; target off-axis 0–45°; 5–9 objects; time per item 45 → 20 s | Level up when mean error < 20° (DJ; typical undergraduate SOT error ≈ 17°, He 2022) | Mean absolute angular error; front/back confusions |
| Span | Length 2–9; crossings 0–3+; forward → backward → board rotated 0–180°; 1.0 → 0.6 s per item | Corsi rule, then 2-down/1-up | Span; product score |
| Hidden | 10–40 segments; 3–5 candidates; near-miss foils one segment off; continuation camouflage | 3-down/1-up | Accuracy, RT |
| Homing | 2 → 4 legs; turns 30–150°; landmarks rich → none; delay 0–10 s | Level steps | Absolute angular and distance error |

Ranges come from the cited studies where they exist; the rest are design judgements to calibrate in a pilot. Log item parameters with every response so later item-response modelling can estimate how much each dimension contributes to difficulty, and recalibrate the levels from that (DJ).
