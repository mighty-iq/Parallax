# Brief A: evidence on spatial-ability training and transfer

Compiled 2026-10-30 for the VSI trainer. Sources: publisher pages, PubMed/PMC, author repositories, Crossref or Semantic Scholar records. Numbers are as reported. "CI from SE" is a 95% CI computed here as estimate ± 1.96·SE. **[unverified]** marks anything not confirmed on a primary page in this session (paywall, blocked page, or recalled). The web-search budget (200 calls) ran out late; the last items were checked by DOI lookup only (§9). ES = effect size; m = studies; k = effect sizes; MR = mental rotation; MRT = Vandenberg Mental Rotations Test; WM = working memory.

## 0. Bottom line
- Spatial practice raises spatial test scores by about 0.4–0.5 SD. Untrained spatial tests gain about as much as trained ones, and gains hold for at least weeks (Uttal et al. 2013).
- Control groups also gain about 0.45 SD (retesting, filler tasks). Against spatial-filler controls the training effect is 0.33. Published effects exceed unpublished ones (0.56 vs 0.39). Only 4 of 206 studies tested later than a month.
- Transfer to maths is smaller (g = 0.28, mostly near transfer; Hawes et al. 2022). Effects on STEM grades are small, and in the one randomised college trial they were gone at 8 months. There is no good evidence of transfer to general intelligence.
- Action-game training: spatial g = 0.45 in Bediou et al. 2018, but 0.26 with a CI spanning zero in the 2023 update.
- The honest claim is in §4.3.

## 1. Malleability
### 1.1 Uttal, Meadow, Tipton, Hand, Alden, Warren & Newcombe 2013, Psychological Bulletin 139(2), 352–402
- The abstract counts 217 studies; analyses use 206 (k = 1,038) after excluding 12 outlier studies (mean g = 1.63). Total N not reported. Random-effects Hedges' g, training vs control.
- **Overall g = 0.47 (SE 0.04; CI from SE 0.39–0.55).** Between-study τ² = 0.185 (SD ≈ 0.43), so single studies vary widely.

- **Durability:** 17 studies tested 1–6 days after training, 37 at 7–31 days, 4 later than a month (excluded); about 67% tested only immediately (Uttal, Miller & Newcombe 2013). Duration could not be tested: very few studies trained for more than a few weeks.
- **Control groups** gained g = 0.45 pre to post (trained groups 0.62), above a 0.29 test-retest benchmark (Hausknecht et al. 2007). Control gain by type: single pre/post test 0.33; repeated practice 0.75 (m = 7); spatial filler 0.51; non-spatial filler 0.37.
- The training effect was 0.33 against spatial-filler controls and 0.56 against non-spatial-filler controls. The authors' reading: strong controls hide part of the training effect and explain some mixed earlier findings.
- **Publication bias:** funnel plot mostly symmetric; Orwin fail-safe N = 762 null studies to bring g to 0.10 (278 for 0.20); no trim-and-fill.
- "Transfer" here means other spatial tests, not school, STEM or IQ outcomes.

## 2. STEM and far transfer
### 2.1 Sorby's spatial-visualisation course (engineering, Michigan Tech)
- **Programme:** first-year students who fail the PSVT:R (Purdue Spatial Visualization Test: Rotations) take a sketching-based course, later a 1-credit, weekly, one-semester course (Sorby 2009, IJSE 31(3), 459–480).
- **PSVT:R, % correct pre → post** (Sorby & Veurink c. 2010): random pilot (n = 24) 51.7 → 82.0; voluntary cohorts (n = 184) 50.5 → 76.9 and (n = 157) 48.3 → 73.7; mandatory (n = 84) 53.6 → 75.4; all p < .0001. Mental Cutting gains were 12–18 points.
- **Later grades,** trained vs comparison (voluntary semester cohort): Calculus I 2.78 vs 2.35; engineering graphics 3.04 vs 2.62. Pilot differences n.s.
- **Retention:** among women who failed the PSVT:R in 1993–98, 77% (69/90) of course takers were still in or had graduated from engineering, vs 48% (77/161) of non-takers; the groups were self-selected (AAUW, Why So Few?, 2010, ch. 5).
- **Regression discontinuity:** Sorby et al. 2013 found a small effect, under 1% of grade variance and a minor calculus gain (via Stieff & Uttal 2015) [primary not read]. Sorby, Veurink & Streiner 2018 (LID 67, 209–222; more than 3,000 students over 5 years, cutoff at pass/fail): positive effects on several introductory courses and STEM GPA (largest in Engineering Problem Solving and Analysis) and on women's retention; magnitudes not retrieved [unverified].
### 2.2 Miller & Halpern 2013, LID 26, 141–152 (physics; the only randomised STEM trial in Stieff & Uttal's review)
- **Design:** N = 77 gifted first-year STEM students randomised (39 training, 38 control); no placebo condition, and trainees were paid $90 more. Training: 12 h (6 × 2 h over 6 weeks) of Sorby–Wysocki sketching modules with snap cubes.
- **About 1 week later:** Mental Cutting d = 0.79, MRT d = 0.35; Paper Folding and Lappan n.s.
- **Physics (Newtonian):** exam composite d = 0.38, course grade d = 0.32, Force Concept Inventory d = −0.02. Chemistry, calculus, differential equations and engineering design n.s.; biology d = 0.45, possibly a multiple-comparison artefact.
- **At 8 months (n = 55):** no training differences on any spatial test (MRT d = −0.09, Mental Cutting d = 0.08), sophomore grades or declared majors.
### 2.3 Stieff and colleagues (chemistry, strategy instruction)
- **Stieff, Dixon, Ryu, Kumi & Hegarty 2014, JEP 106(2), 390–402:** organic-chemistry course supplements trained imagery strategies, analytic strategies, or both. Analytic training raised analytic strategy use, and women then used analytic strategies more than men. Combined training eliminated the sex gap in achievement; single-strategy training left a male advantage.
- Stieff 2014: N, dose and ES not retrieved [unverified]. An AERA 2012 paper, apparently from the same project, lists 514 students (analytic 157, imagistic 159, combined 198), each with three 1-h sessions and three assignments [presenter not listed].
- **Stieff & Uttal 2015, EPR 27, 607–615:** training reliably improves spatial skill, but causal evidence for STEM grades, retention or degrees is still missing. Only six training-to-STEM-achievement studies in 30 years, mostly quasi-experimental (e.g. Small & Morton 1983, chemistry: about 3 points on one assessment, no grade difference). They call for RCTs or active matched controls, measured spatial change, time-on-task covariates and delayed tests.
### 2.4 Lowrie et al. (maths) and other child trials
- **Lowrie, Logan & Ramful 2017, BJEP 87(2), 170–186:** grade 6, N = 186 (5 classes, n = 120, vs 3 control classes, n = 66), quasi-experimental; 20 h over 10 weeks replaced maths lessons. Spatial and maths gains vs control (ES not on the abstract page).
- **Lowrie, Logan & Hegarty 2019, JCD 20(5), 729–751:** N = 327 in 17 classes (9 vs 8), 3 weeks. Gains in visualisation, orientation, geometry and word problems; not in non-geometric graphics.
- **Lowrie, Harris, Logan & Hegarty 2021, J Exp Educ 89(2), 259–277:** grade 8, 40 classes (32 vs 8), 12 h over 10 weeks, hierarchical linear models. Gains in spatial reasoning, geometry/measurement and number/algebra.
- **Lowrie & Logan 2023, J Intell 11(6):127** (cluster RCT, grade 4, N = 287: control 97, digital-only 98, embedded programme 92): on trained skills (η² = .029) the embedded programme beat both groups and digital-only did not beat control; on untrained spatial skills (η² = .023) and maths (η² = .027) both programmes beat control, on maths by about 0.66 of 16 items.
- **Cheng & Mix 2014, JCD 15(1), 2–11:** N = 58, ages 6–8, one 40-min MR session vs crosswords. MR η² = .23; maths η² = .14, driven by missing-term problems (η² = .12).
- **Cheung, Sung & Lourenco 2020, Psychol Res 84(7), 2000–2017:** N = 62, ages 6–7, one week of at-home MR training vs a literacy active control. MR and WJ-III Calculation improved; non-symbolic quantity, verbal WM and language did not (ES not retrieved).
- **Gilligan, Thomas & Farran 2020, Dev Sci e12909:** N = 250 eight-year-olds; MR or spatial-scaling training, explicit (videos) or implicit (practice with feedback). Near, intermediate and maths transfer; explicit and implicit equally effective except for geometry.
- **Nulls:** Rodán et al. 2019 (LID 70, 1–11; about 92 second-graders, 450 MR trials over 3 sessions): MR ηp² = .10, no maths gain. Hawes et al. 2015: no maths transfer, as cited by Lowrie & Logan 2023 [not checked].
### 2.5 Prediction, not training: Wai, Lubinski & Benbow 2009, JEP 101(4), 817–835
- Project TALENT: about 400,000 US students in grades 9–12 (1960), followed for 11+ years. 45% of later STEM PhDs had been in the top 4% (stanine 9) for spatial ability, and nearly 90% in the top 23% (stanine 7 or higher).
- Spatial ability added about 4% of variance (ΔR² ≈ .04) beyond maths and verbal ability in predicting STEM vs non-STEM outcomes; about 70% of the top 1% in spatial ability were not in the top 1% for maths or verbal. Correlational: it says nothing about training.
### 2.6 Verdict on far transfer
- **Strongest evidence:** Sorby's large regression-discontinuity study (grades, women's retention; quasi-experimental, valid near the cutoff, small where magnitudes are reported); Hawes' meta-analytic maths g = 0.28 (near > far, no detectable bias); several controlled child studies (N = 58–287), some randomised.
- **Limits:** nulls (Rodán 2019; Hawes 2015); the adult RCT faded by 8 months; small samples (game-training arms typically 13–45; Bediou 2018); passive or non-spatial controls inflate effects (Uttal: 0.56 vs 0.33); publication bias (0.56 vs 0.39); almost no follow-up beyond a month; expectations uncontrolled in most designs (§4).

## 3. Video games
- **Feng, Spence & Pratt 2007, Psych Sci 18(10), 850–855:** 10 h of an action game (Medal of Honor: Pacific Assault) vs a 3D puzzle game (Ballance). Spatial attention and MR improved, women more than men; the sex gap in spatial attention almost vanished and the MR gap narrowed; controls did not improve. Small sample [N and follow-up unverified].
- **Terlecki, Newcombe & Little 2008, ACP 22(7), 996–1013:** undergraduates took the MRT repeatedly or played Tetris (1 h/week for 12 weeks, Solitaire control, per Adams & Mayer 2012) [N not retrieved]. Both gave large gains lasting months; men improved faster early, women later, and scores did not converge.
- Terlecki 2008, continued: Tetris grew faster early but ended level with repeated testing, and transferred more to other spatial tests (Guilford–Zimmerman, Surface Development) for several months. Low-scoring women started slowly, so short studies underestimate their gains (Uttal et al. 2013).
- **Bediou, Adams, Mayer, Tipton, Green & Bavelier 2018, Psych Bull 144(1), 77–110:** cross-sectional (gamers vs non-gamers) g = 0.55 [0.42, 0.68] (k = 194, m = 89). Interventions (at least 8 h over at least 8 days, active non-action-game control; 10–50 h, median 23) g = 0.34 [0.09, 0.59] (k = 90, m = 22); **spatial cognition 0.45 [0.12, 0.77] (k = 29, m = 9)**; top-down attention 0.31 [0.14, 0.48].
- Bediou 2018, continued: lab of origin was the only significant moderator (Bavelier lab g ≈ 1.0, k = 18; others ≈ 0.2) and absorbed the effect of training duration. Egger's test was significant; trim-and-fill cut estimates by 28–32%. Samples were typically 13–45.
- **Bediou, Rodgers, Tipton, Mayer, Green & Bavelier 2023, Technology, Mind, and Behavior 4(1):** abstract: 28 active-control intervention studies (91 ES); analysed: 83 ES from 18 independent samples (N = 2,739). Overall g = 0.30 [0.11, 0.50]; **spatial cognition 0.26 [−0.27, 0.79] (k = 15, m = 6, p = .265, n.s.)**; only top-down attention was significant (0.52 [0.25, 0.79]).
- Bediou 2023, continued: cross-sectional overall 0.64 [0.53, 0.74], spatial 0.67 [0.45, 0.90]. No significant publication bias among interventions (selection-model estimate 0.33). Duration (8–50 h) n.s. in the full model. Nine 2018 studies were dropped under new criteria.
- **Sala, Tatlidil & Gobet 2018, Psych Bull 144(2), 111–139:** training g = 0.07 [0.02, 0.12] (k = 359, N = 3,286), trim-and-fill −0.01 [−0.06, 0.05]. Game skill correlates with spatial ability at r = .18 [.13, .23] (k = 50); gamers beat non-gamers on spatial tests by g = 0.24 [0.13, 0.34] (k = 28). A spatial-only training estimate was not retrieved.
- **Green & Bavelier 2012, Current Biology 22(6), R197–R206:** features credited with transfer: speed and pacing; high perceptual, cognitive and motor load; attention split between centre and periphery; constant prediction; continuous feedback; rewards on many time scales. Proposed mechanism: better attentional control, which speeds new learning ("learning to learn"); varied training generalises more.
- **Takeaway:** gamers outscore non-gamers on spatial tests, but game training has not been shown to cause spatial gains under the 2023 inclusion rules and lab controls. Tetris-like practice improves rotation and some untrained spatial tests.

## 4. The sceptical side
### 4.1 General cognitive training
- **Sala & Gobet 2017, Curr Dir Psych Sci 26(6), 515–520:** chess, music and WM training effects are small and shrink with active controls; far transfer rarely occurs, and they extend the argument to spatial training, brain training and games.
- **Sala & Gobet 2019, Trends Cogn Sci 23(1), 9–20:** effects on domain-general skills are minimal; design quality and artefacts explain between-study variation. Spatial training gets one mention (citing Xu & LeFevre 2016; Mix & Cheng 2012): no appreciable effect on overall cognition, but few studies.
- **Sala, Aksayli, Tatlidil, Tatsumi, Gondo & Gobet 2019, Collabra 5(1):18** (second-order meta-analysis): WM training shows near transfer (k = 99); far transfer is small or null (k = 119; k = 233 with games, music, chess, exergames); with placebo and publication bias controlled, the far-transfer effect and its true variance were both zero.
- **Simons, Boot, Charness, Gathercole, Chabris, Hambrick & Stine-Morrow 2016, PSPI 17(3), 103–186:** extensive evidence for trained tasks, less for closely related tasks, little for distant tasks or everyday life. Frequent flaws: passive controls, controls with different expectations, small samples, selective reporting, duplicate publication.
- **Foroughi, Monfort, Paczynski, McKnight & Greenwood 2016, PNAS 113(27), 7470–7474:** N = 50 did the same 1 h of dual n-back, recruited with a suggestive brain-training flyer or a neutral one. Only the suggestive group gained (RAPM d = 0.50, BOMAT d = 0.39 within group; 5–10 IQ points by the authors' extrapolation).
### 4.2 Critiques specific to spatial training
- Controls gain 0.45 SD (repeated-practice controls 0.75); published > unpublished (0.56 vs 0.39); within-subject designs inflate effects (0.75); about two thirds of studies tested only immediately.
- The game-to-spatial effect is not robust (2023 update, lab effect, Sala 2018 near zero); causal STEM evidence is lacking (Stieff & Uttal 2015); I found no spatial study that measured or matched participants' expectations [gap].
### 4.3 Honest one-sentence summary
> Practising spatial tasks reliably improves spatial test performance, including on untrained tests, for at least several weeks; gains in maths are small (shown mainly in children and teenagers), gains in STEM grades are small and not firmly established, and there is no good evidence of higher general intelligence, so this app measures your transfer instead of promising it.

Short UI version: *Spatial practice reliably improves spatial test performance, even on untrained tests, for weeks; benefits beyond that are small or unproven, so we measure your transfer rather than promise it.*

## 6. Structure of the target ability
### 6.1 Wechsler Visual Spatial Index (VSI)
- **WISC-V (ages 6–16):** VSI = Block Design (BD) + Visual Puzzles (VP); five primary indexes from 10 primary subtests, no substitutions (Canivez & Watkins 2016).
- **WAIS-5 (Pearson 2024, ages 16–90:11):** VSI = BD + VP. Other primary indexes: Verbal Comprehension (Similarities, Vocabulary), Fluid Reasoning (Matrix Reasoning, Figure Weights), Working Memory (Digit Sequencing, Running Digits), Processing Speed (Coding, Symbol Search). Full Scale IQ: 7 subtests, about 45 min; inferred from the sample report's sums as Similarities, Vocabulary, BD, Matrix Reasoning, Figure Weights, Digit Sequencing and Coding [not stated by Pearson].
- **WAIS-5 ancillary:** Expanded Visual Spatial = BD, VP, Symbol Span, Spatial Addition; Visual Working Memory = Symbol Span + Spatial Addition; Nonverbal = BD, VP, Matrix Reasoning, Figure Weights, Symbol Span, Coding; General Ability = Similarities, Vocabulary, BD, Matrix Reasoning, Figure Weights. New vs WAIS-IV: Running Digits, Set Relations, Naming Speed Quantity, Symbol Span, Spatial Addition; Picture Completion and Cancellation are gone.
- **Task demands** [unverified: general knowledge]: BD asks the examinee to copy two-colour patterns with cubes under time limits; VP asks which 3 pieces make up a target figure, timed. Uttal's 2x2 classes BD as intrinsic-dynamic.
- **Structural caveat** (Canivez & Watkins 2016, WISC-V): the publisher's Fluid Reasoning index loads 1.00 on g; VSI omega-subscale = .144, so most reliable VSI variance is shared with g; exploratory analysis put BD, VP, Matrix Reasoning and Figure Weights on one factor. A claim to raise VSI is in effect a claim about a g-saturated composite.
### 6.2 CHC Gv narrow abilities
Source: Schneider & McGrew 2018; definitions via McGrew's IAP list of 2017-07-20, abstracted from that chapter.
- **Gv** (broad): using simulated mental imagery to solve problems: perceiving, discriminating and manipulating images.
- **Vz Visualisation:** perceive complex patterns and mentally simulate how they would look when transformed (rotated, resized, partly hidden).
- **SR Speeded rotation:** rotate simple images quickly; the fast variant of Vz.
- **IM Imagery:** form vivid images of absent objects or events at will.
- **CS Closure speed:** quickly recognise a familiar object from incomplete or obscured cues, without knowing in advance what it is.
- **CF Flexibility of closure:** find a known figure hidden in a complex, distracting pattern.
- **MV Visual memory:** hold complex images over short intervals (under about 30 s).
- **SS Spatial scanning:** quickly survey a wide or complicated field to find a target or a path.
- **PI Serial perceptual integration:** recognise an object shown as rapid successive parts.
- **LE Length estimation:** estimate lengths by eye, without tools.
- **IL Perceptual illusions:** resist being fooled by visual illusions.
- **PN Perceptual alternations:** keep a consistent rate of switching between alternative percepts.
- **Placed outside Gv:** perceptual speed (P) sits under processing speed (Gs), with a secondary Gv loading. Visual-spatial short-term storage (Wv) and attentional control (AC) sit under working memory (Gwm).
### 6.3 Typologies
- **2x2 (Uttal et al. 2013; Newcombe & Shipley 2015):** intrinsic = one object's parts and shape; extrinsic = relations among objects or to a frame of reference; static = no transformation; dynamic = the object is moved or transformed, physically or mentally.
- Example tests (Uttal Table 1): intrinsic-static: Embedded Figures, closure tasks, mazes. Intrinsic-dynamic: Form Board, BD, Paper Folding, Mental Cutting, MRT, Cube Comparison, PSVT, Card Rotations. Extrinsic-static: Water Level, Plumb Line, Rod and Frame. Extrinsic-dynamic: Three Mountains, Guilford–Zimmerman orientation.
- **Mix, Hambrick, Satyam, Burgoyne & Levine 2018, Cognition 180, 268–278** (CFA; kindergarten N = 251, grade 3 N = 246, grade 6 N = 241): the 4-factor 2x2 model did not converge; intrinsic vs extrinsic fit best in kindergarten and grade 3; in grade 6 nothing beat a single factor. Use the 2x2 as a coverage checklist, not a measurement model.
- **Buckley, Seery & Canty 2018, EPR 30(3), 947–972:** a heuristic framework of up to about 25 factors. Adds spatial orientation (perspective taking) as its own factor and "spatial relations" (untimed rotation of complex 3D figures, possibly within Vz); proposes dynamic factors (direction and speed judgement, movement detection, arrival-time and interception judgement) and hypothetical dynamic versions of MV, SS, PI and PN.
- Buckley et al., continued: also adds imagery quality and speed, illusion subfactors, and small- vs large-scale ability (large-scale being a blend); excludes self-report imagery and knowledge-dependent factors. Full factor table not retrieved.
### 6.4 3D multiple-object tracking (3D-MOT)
- **Not a Gv narrow ability.** Individual differences in MOT relate to attention and WM: correlations with task switching and WM (Oksama & Hyönä 2004); loading on a general visual-attention factor (Huang, Mo & Li 2012); both via Meyerhoff, Papenmeier & Huff 2017, coefficients not retrieved.
- **Fluid intelligence:** in Tullo, Faubert & Bertone 2018 (Intelligence), 3D-MOT speed thresholds at the 4-target load best predicted WASI-II fluid reasoning; r and N not retrieved [magnitude unverified].
- **Gv:** Trick, Mutreja & Hunt 2012 reportedly found that spatial and visuospatial WM tests, but not non-spatial executive tests, predict MOT [unverified, recalled]. I found no verified MOT–Gv coefficient.
- **Training:** Harris, Wilson, Smith, Meder & Vine 2020 (N = 84, randomised, 5 sessions of about 30 min): the trained task improved; no transfer to a different MOT task; n-back d = 0.62–1.11 but baselines mismatched; no far transfer. Vater, Gray & Holcombe 2021 (NeuroTracker review): very weak evidence for WM or attention gains; only 3 studies tested far transfer and 2 were null; none preregistered.

### 7.2 Wechsler retest data
- **WAIS-IV** retest (N = 298, 8–82 days, mean 22): practice effects were largest for Perceptual Reasoning subtests, including BD, VP, Figure Weights and Picture Completion (Canivez 2010 review; magnitudes not given).
- **Estevis, Basso & Combs 2012** (WAIS-IV, N = 54, 3 or 6 months): gains of about 7 points on Full Scale IQ, 5 on Perceptual Reasoning, 9 on Processing Speed, 5 on Verbal Comprehension, 4 on Working Memory and 6 on General Ability; the authors read these as practice effects, not real change [abstract read on a scite listing; attribution by matching].
- **Older WAIS** (N = 76 male students): Performance IQ +11.4 at 1 week, +9.8 at 1 month, +8.7 at 2 months, +8.0 at 4 months; Verbal IQ +4.7 falling to +0.8 [authors and year not retrieved].
- **Kaufman & Lichtenberger 2006** (secondary source): Performance IQ gains of about 8–8.5 points at ages 16–54, about twice the Verbal IQ gains; BD gains come mostly from faster responding.
- **WISC-V clinical sample** (N = 225, mean interval 2.6 years): no gain (BD −0.22 scaled points, VP +0.01, VSI −0.42); stability r = .68, .75 and .82 [authors not retrieved].
### 7.3 Does practising test-like items raise scores without changing the ability?
- The evidence points to yes. Gains are larger with identical forms and plateau by the 3rd administration; Scharfen et al. judge latent change improbable; te Nijenhuis, van Vianen & van der Flier 2007 ("Score gains on g-loaded tests: No g") argue the gains are not on g [coefficients not retrieved].
- BD gains come largely from speed; half of the MRT's foils can be rejected using orientation-independent features (Hegarty 2018), a test-specific shortcut; MR training moves response time (g = 0.69) more than accuracy (0.31); expectation alone raised matrix scores (Foroughi 2016).
- I found no study that trained BD-like items and then measured Wechsler VSI.
### 7.4 Implication
- **Yes, avoid cloning Wechsler formats (BD, VP),** and keep any test you plan to use as an outcome (MRT, PSVT:R, Paper Folding) out of training. Training on them would inflate exactly the scores used to judge transfer, probably without moving the latent ability, and would invite "my VSI went up" claims. Test security and publisher copyright are a further reason [my inference].

## 8. Design implications
1. **Claims:** use §4.3. Never claim gains in IQ, VSI, g or STEM grades. Report each user's transfer estimate with its uncertainty.
2. **Separate training from assessment:** the transfer battery uses formats never trained, covers all four 2x2 cells, and uses alternate forms. No BD, VP, MRT or PSVT:R clones in training.
3. **Baselines:** run the battery 2–3 times before training (retest gain 0.33 at the 2nd test, +0.17 at the 3rd, then flat). Compare later change with a retest curve (untrained users or alternate forms), not with zero.
4. **Controls:** where possible, randomise users to an active non-spatial arm or a delayed start. Keep messaging neutral and log expected improvement at baseline as a covariate, since expectations alone raised matrix scores. Report response time and accuracy separately.
5. **Follow-up:** delayed probes at about 1 month and at 3–6 months, where the literature is thinnest.
6. **Content:** varied tasks across the 2x2 cells and the CHC narrow abilities (Vz, SR, CF, SS, MV, perspective taking), plus dynamic tasks (motion, interception).
7. **Strategy:** teach imagery and analytic strategies and when to switch between them. Prompt brief strategy reports to track flexibility.
8. **Feedback:** after each item, immediately animate the correct transformation (rotation, fold) and let users manipulate the 3D object. Make sure the informative views are actually seen; interaction alone may not help.
9. **Embodiment:** training without physical materials (e.g. computerised) gave smaller maths transfer on average (Hawes 2022; Gilligan-Lee 2023). Offer optional offline tasks with cubes or paper, and gesture prompts (gesture evidence [unverified]).
10. **Dose and pacing:** about 10–20 h over 6–12 weeks, in short sessions. Spacing is untested, so make it adjustable and log it. Tell slow starters that early plateaus are common (Terlecki).
11. **Analysis:** expect larger gains at low baselines, but correct for regression to the mean by averaging several baseline sessions.
12. **3D-MOT,** if included, is labelled attention and WM practice, not VSI training.

## 9. Gaps and items not verified here
- Not checked because the search budget ran out: Karaçalı Taze 2026 results; the Hegarty 2010 chapter text; Chu & Kita 2011; Keehner 2008 details; Trick 2012, Tullo 2018 and te Nijenhuis 2007 coefficients; Sorby 2018 effect sizes; Stieff 2014 N and ES; Feng 2007 N and follow-up; Terlecki 2008 N; VSI retest gains in the WISC-V and WAIS-5 manuals.
- I found no primary spatial-training study that isolates feedback, spacing or expectations.
