# D. Motivation, gamification, onboarding and feedback: research notes
Scope: free, open-source browser trainer for visuospatial tasks (3D-MOT, mental rotation, paper folding, cube nets, cross-sections, perspective taking); adults and some teens, many on phones. Compiled 2026-10-30.
Tags: [unverified] = not confirmed against a primary source in this session; [secondary] = figure taken from a secondary summary; (inference) = our design reasoning, not a finding. The web-search budget ran out part-way, so a few points rest on a single source.

## 1. Self-determination theory (SDT) and rewards
- Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski 2006, four studies: N=89 (Super Mario 64), N=50 (Zelda vs A Bug's Life), N=58 (four games, multilevel), N=730 MMO players. Perceived autonomy and competence predicted enjoyment and wanting to play again (Study 1: enjoyment beta=.49 for autonomy, .34 for competence); only competence predicted actually choosing to keep playing (beta=.41). Intuitive controls mattered only through need satisfaction (mainly competence). In Study 4 relatedness added unique variance to enjoyment and future play (beta=.12 each), and the "achievement" play motive predicted worse post-play mood (beta=-.21).
- The authors' design advice (theory, not tested there): optimal challenge, positive informational feedback, choice of tasks and goals, controls that are easy to master.
- Cognitive evaluation theory: feedback or rewards experienced as informational (they tell you about your competence) support intrinsic motivation; experienced as controlling (pressure to behave a certain way) they undermine it.
- Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 (128 experiments): positive verbal feedback raised free-choice persistence (d=+0.33) and interest (d=+0.31); on free choice the effect held for college students (d=+0.43) but not children (d=+0.11, n.s.). Expected tangible rewards lowered free-choice behaviour (d=-0.36): engagement-contingent -0.40, completion-contingent -0.36, performance-contingent -0.28; unexpected rewards had no effect (d=0.01). Tangible rewards harmed children more than students. Two cited studies found controlling feedback produced less intrinsic motivation than informational feedback.
- So (inference): praise that carries information is safe; rewards for showing up or for time on the app are the riskiest kind; younger users gain less from praise and lose more from reward systems.

## 2. Gamification evidence
- Sailer & Homner 2020 meta-analysis: cognitive outcomes g=0.49 (k=19, N=1,686), motivational g=0.36 (k=16, N=2,246), behavioural g=0.25 (k=9, N=951). In the methodologically rigorous subset only the cognitive effect held (g=0.42); motivational (0.22) and behavioural (0.27) were n.s. Competition combined with collaboration beat no social interaction for behaviour, and in the rigorous subset beat competition alone for motivation and behaviour. Game fiction helped behavioural outcomes only.
- Bai, Hew & Huang 2020: learning outcomes g=0.504 [0.28, 0.72] (30 interventions, 24 studies, N=3,202); type and number of game elements did not moderate. Qualitative synthesis (32 studies): learners liked the enthusiasm, performance feedback, recognition and help with goal-setting; they disliked lack of clear utility, anxiety and jealousy.
- Koivisto & Hamari 2019: 819 papers reviewed (273 empirical studies in depth); points, badges and leaderboards dominate; results lean positive but many are mixed; theory and measures are inconsistent across the field.
- Sailer et al. 2017 (randomised online task, N=331 analysed): badges + leaderboard + performance graphs raised perceived competence (partial eta squared .020) and task meaningfulness (.018) over points only; avatars + story + teammates raised social relatedness (.032); nothing raised decision freedom. 88 of 419 completers had not noticed their condition's elements at all.
- Badges: Hamari 2017 field experiment on a peer-to-peer trading service (1,410 users without badges vs 1,579 with, one year each): badge users posted more trade proposals, made more transactions and comments, and viewed more pages. Hamari 2013, a similar trading service: gains appeared only among users who actively checked badges.
- Leaderboards and points: Mekler et al. 2017 found points, levels and leaderboards raised quantity of output but not intrinsic motivation or perceived competence [unverified: abstract not retrieved]; Hanus & Fox 2015 (16-week course) found a badge + leaderboard section lost intrinsic motivation and satisfaction and scored lower on the final exam than a plain section, partly via social comparison [unverified: abstract not retrieved]; Landers et al. 2017 suggest leaderboards act as goals, so easy implied goals help less [as cited by Sailer & Homner 2020].
- Kluger & DeNisi 1996 (607 effects, 23,663 observations): feedback helps on average (d=0.41) but made performance worse in over a third of cases, typically when it pulls attention away from the task towards the self (comparison with others, praise or threat aimed at the person).
- Goal gradient (Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng 2006): on 10-stamp coffee cards the gap between purchases shrank ~20% (0.7 days) as the reward approached; a 12-stamp card with 2 free stamps (still 10 purchases) was finished in 12.7 vs 15.6 days. On a song-rating site (148 members) effort rose near the goal, visits most often ended right after a reward (17% of terminations), and effort dipped after each reward ("post-reward resetting").
- Endowed progress (Nunes & Dreze 2006): an artificial head start raises completion (car-wash cards ~34% vs 19%) [unverified numbers]. A fake head start would be dishonest in this app.
- Habit timing (Lally et al. 2010): 96 volunteers, 84 days of daily reports; in the 39 with good model fit, median 66 days to reach 95% of their own automaticity plateau (range 18-254, partly extrapolated); one missed day barely changed automaticity [details secondary; consistent with the UCL press release].
- Streaks (Silverman & Barasch 2023, 7 studies): an intact logged streak raises later engagement relative to a broken one, even when actual behaviour is identical; the break hurts more when people blame themselves and less when the streak can be repaired. In one study 66% vs 58% chose to continue the behaviour [secondary]; some participants paid a cost (watching an ad) to repair a streak.
- Aulagnon et al. 2026 (working paper; RCT, N=60,000 Peruvian pupils, summer maths app): weekly-streak messages raised the share of weeks used among users by 9.4 pp (control 31.7%), with no sign that broken streaks led to longer absences; the learning gain (0.13-0.17 SD) rests on 2.3% endline test take-up.
- Duolingo (company claims, not peer reviewed): letting one lesson extend the streak, separately from the daily goal, raised day-14 retention by 3.3% and learners on a streak by 10.5%, but fewer learners met their daily goals (2020 blog). Podcast claims: two streak freezes beat one, three were no better; a free "earn back" repair helps; a reminder 23.5 h after the last lesson and a 10 pm "streak saver" [unverified; secondary summary]. Khan Academy reportedly retired streaks over demotivation concerns (cited by Aulagnon et al.) [unverified].

## 3. Cognitive-training claims and ethics
- FTC v. Lumos Labs (5 Jan 2016): alleged deceptive claims that Lumosity improves everyday, school, work and athletic performance; delays age-related decline and protects against MCI, dementia and Alzheimer's; reduces impairment from stroke, TBI, PTSD, ADHD, chemotherapy and Turner syndrome; and that science proved all this. Website testimonials came from contests with undisclosed prizes. A $50M judgment was suspended on payment of $2M; future claims need competent and reliable scientific evidence. Press coverage quotes the FTC saying the firm preyed on fears of age-related decline [unverified].
- Commissioner Brill's concurring statement: do not overstate benefits or imply that in-game gains carry over to real life; implied claims count; science-sounding claims can suggest more than the evidence supports.
- FTC v. Carrot Neurotechnology (Ultimeyes app, Sept 2015): "scientifically shown to improve vision" was unsubstantiated, and a co-owner's studies were promoted without disclosing his stake; $150,000.
- FTC Health Products Compliance Guidance (Dec 2022): covers any health-related claim, apps included; judged by net impression including implied claims (a brain claim can imply a dementia claim); needs RCT-quality evidence matching the product, dose, population and outcome; "may" or "helps" does not cure an unsupported claim; limitations must be clear, conspicuous and not behind a link; testimonials need typical-results context and disclosed connections.
- Stanford Center on Longevity / Max Planck consensus (20 Oct 2014, ~69 signatories): marketing exaggerates; gains are narrow, often small and may fade; little evidence of broad transfer or better brain health; consider the opportunity cost (exercise, social life); no evidence that games prevent dementia. Rebuttal letter (Cognitive Training Data, 17 Dec 2014, 127 signatories at release) cited RCTs showing benefits but agreed that marketing is often exaggerated.
- Simons et al. 2016 (PSPI): extensive evidence that trained tasks improve; less evidence for closely related tasks; little for distant tasks or everyday performance; no study met all best practices (active controls, preregistration, adequate samples, full reporting).
- Spatial training: Uttal et al. 2013 (217 studies) found g=0.47, durable, transferring to untrained spatial tasks (g=0.48), but control groups also gained g=0.45 from retesting and practice. Wright et al. 2008 (N=31; 15-20 min/day for 21 days): mental-rotation and paper-folding practice each transferred to the other, beyond a verbal control task.
- 3D-MOT: Vater, Gray & Holcombe 2021 reviewed 29 NeuroTracker studies (16 interventions, 10 of minimum quality): mixed near transfer and no preregistered studies; of 3 far-transfer tests, 2 were null (volleyball, driving) and 1 positive with n=7 per group; marketing claims exceed the evidence.

## 4. Tutorials and onboarding
- Andersen et al. 2012 (CHI; >45,000 new players randomised to 8 tutorial conditions in Refraction, Hello Worlds and Foldit): tutorials paid off only in the complex game (Foldit: context-sensitive tutorials +75% levels and +29% play time vs none; up-front manual +25% and +12%). Just-in-time beat up-front there (+40% levels, +16% time) but not in the simple games. Forcing exact actions ("blocking" stencils) gave no benefit. An on-demand help button was mixed and hurt Refraction (-12% levels, -15% time; only 31% used it). Players of simple games learned by experimenting; the authors advise discovery-friendly early levels, information at the moment of need, and A/B tests because tutorials can backfire. They tested only text and pictures, not animation or video.
- Mayer: pretraining on the names and features of key parts (7/7 experiments, median d=0.92) and learner-paced segments (3/3, median d=0.98) in the 2005 handbook; later tallies 16 tests d=0.75 and 10 tests d=0.79 [secondary]. Rey et al. 2019 segmenting meta-analysis (56 investigations, 88 comparisons): small-to-medium gains for retention and transfer, also for system-paced segments.
- Hoffler & Leutner 2007 (26 studies, 76 comparisons): animation beat static pictures, d=0.37; representational animation (showing the content itself) d=0.40 vs decorative -0.05; procedural-motor knowledge d=1.06. So a short demo of how a trial works is the best-supported use (inference). Berney & Betrancourt 2016 (~140 comparisons): smaller gains (procedural g~0.39, conceptual g~0.16) [secondary].
- Expertise reversal (Kalyuga et al. 2003): guidance that helps novices can hinder experienced users, so fade it and allow skipping. Chu et al. 2017 (N=410, mental-rotation tool): a colour-cue + 3D-animation strategy helped low-ability students (gain 1.87 vs 1.24) and hurt high-ability ones (-1.06 vs -0.54).

## 5. Animation and feedback for spatial learning
- Tversky, Morrison & Betrancourt 2002: many apparent animation benefits were confounded with extra information or interactivity. To help, animations must be slow and clear enough to perceive (apprehension), match the concept (congruence), mark key changes with arrows or highlights, emphasise changes in relations, and offer pause, replay, speed and viewpoint control.
- Signalling (Schneider et al. 2018): retention g~0.53, transfer g~0.33; flashing cues (3 studies) g~-0.56, n.s. [secondary]. Steady outlines, not blinking.
- Showing the correct transformation:
  - Cohen & Hegarty 2014: low-spatial undergraduates did 6-12 min of interactive cross-section animations (draw, self-check, corrective feedback) and beat reading controls on trained figures (Exp 1 N=20, Exp 2 N=22), and in Exp 2 also on untrained figures.
  - Keehner et al. 2008: interactivity itself did not improve cross-section inference; seeing the most task-relevant views did. Passive viewers of good rotations matched effective interactors and beat poor ones.
  - James et al. 2002 (N=22, immersive VR): actively rotated objects were later recognised faster (about 2,442 vs 2,588 ms by item) but not more accurately; people dwelt on front, side and back ("plan") views. Harman et al. 1999: active control speeds later recognition.
  - We found no clean experiment isolating animated vs static corrective feedback for mental rotation (a 1996 thesis found no animated-vs-static difference, and its repository entry is marked withdrawn). Animated solution replays are plausible but rest on adjacent evidence.
- Feedback content (Wisniewski et al. 2020; 435 studies, 994 effects): overall d=0.48; reinforcement or punishment (praise, rewards) d=0.24; corrective (right/wrong plus the correct answer) d=0.46; high-information (task plus process: why, and how to do it better) d=0.99.
- Timing and need in perceptual learning: feedback delayed by 2.5 s or more impaired procedural ("information-integration") category learning, while rule-based learning tolerated 10 s (Maddox, Ashby & Bohil 2003; Maddox & Ing 2005) [via Valentin et al. 2014]; a model paper reports ~0.5 s as best (Valentin et al. 2014). Liu, Lu & Dosher 2010 (N=24): at 85% accuracy learning occurred with or without feedback (27.5% vs 23.2% threshold drop); at 65% only with feedback (33.2% vs 0.7%). Herzog & Fahle 1997: hard stimuli needed feedback and false feedback blocked learning [secondary, via Liu et al.]. Wilson et al. 2019: for gradient-descent learners on binary tasks, ~85% training accuracy maximises learning rate (theory and simulation; human evidence limited).

## 6. Session design and adherence
- Lampit et al. 2014 (51 RCTs, healthy older adults): overall g=0.22; home-based g=0.09 (n.s.) vs supervised groups 0.29; 1x/week g=0.34, 2-3x/week 0.28, >3x/week 0.07 (n.s.); evidence that sessions under 30 min fail is weak. More frequent was not better.
- Wright et al. 2008: 15-20 min a day for 21 days; 7 of 38 did not finish. Molloy et al. 2012 (auditory perceptual learning): ~8-min sessions gave faster early learning than >1-hour sessions; one hour in one session matched four hours over four sessions in long-term gains.
- Singh et al. 2022 (Harrell et al. trial; N=118 older adults, 12 weeks, 45 min/day on 5 days a week): adherence slipped after day 30; message framing did not help; the authors propose personalised just-in-time reminders.
- ICO Children's Code (UK, under-18s): avoid data-driven features that make it hard to stop; present "keep playing" choices neutrally; let users pause at any time without losing progress.
- No trials found on end-of-session summaries or personal-best framing; the advice below follows task-focused, self-referenced feedback (Kluger & DeNisi), performance graphs (Sailer et al. 2017) and natural stopping after a goal (Kivetz et al.).

## 7. Accessibility facts
- WCAG 2.3.1 (Level A): no more than three flashes in any one second unless below thresholds. A general flash is a swing of at least 10% in relative luminance where the darker state is below 0.80, over a combined area above 0.006 sr (~25% of a 10-degree field, ~341x256 px on a 1024x768 screen); any transition to or from saturated red (R/(R+G+B) >= 0.8, defined in WCAG 2.2) counts as a red flash. Phones held close make even "compliant" areas larger in the visual field.
- Epilepsy Foundation: ~3% of people with epilepsy are photosensitive, most often children and adolescents; flicker at 5-30 Hz is most provocative; high-contrast stripes can also trigger seizures.
- Vestibular: up to 35% of US adults aged 40+ have had vestibular dysfunction [secondary]; motion the user did not start (parallax, large moving scenes) can cause dizziness and nausea. WCAG 2.3.3 (AAA): interaction-triggered motion can be switched off unless essential. prefers-reduced-motion (CSS and matchMedia, widely supported since 2020) reflects the OS "reduce motion" setting on Windows, macOS, iOS and Android.
- WCAG 2.2.2 (A): moving content that starts automatically and lasts over 5 s needs pause/stop/hide unless essential (games can offer pause). WCAG 2.2.1 (A): time limits can be turned off, or adjusted or extended at least tenfold, unless essential.
- WCAG 2.1.1 (A): everything works by keyboard, without timing-dependent keystrokes. WCAG 2.5.7 (AA): anything done by dragging also works with single taps or clicks.
- Target size: WCAG 2.5.8 (AA) at least 24x24 CSS px or enough spacing; WCAG 2.5.5 (AAA) at least 44x44; Android guidance 48x48 dp (~9 mm) with 8 dp gaps.
- Colour: WCAG 1.4.1 (A) colour is never the only cue; WCAG 1.4.11 (AA) 3:1 contrast for controls, states, focus rings and needed graphics. Red-green deficiency affects ~8% of men of European descent, 5% of Asian and 4% of African men (Okabe & Ito). Okabe-Ito palette: #E69F00, #56B4E9, #009E73, #F0E442, #0072B2, #D55E00, #CC79A7 [hex values from common reproductions, unverified against the source figure].

## 8. Relatedness without leaderboards
- Relatedness predicts enjoyment and return (Ryan et al. 2006); teammates and story raise it (Sailer et al. 2017); competition mixed with collaboration beats competition alone (Sailer & Homner 2020); comparison with others is the riskiest feedback (Kluger & DeNisi 1996); learners report jealousy and anxiety (Bai et al. 2020). We found no studies of shareable result cards or seeded challenges as such; the ideas below are inference.

## Recommendations
These are design inferences from the evidence above; the citations name the main support.
### (a) Beginner tutorial: Watch, Try, Go (about 60 s per task)
Order: the required Chimera welcome dialog comes first on every open (short; focus inside; Esc or one button closes it; Discord link clickable). On the first run, closing it offers "New here? 60-second tour" with Start and Skip.
1. A goal line of at most 12 words ("Same object, or a mirror image?") and 2-3 labelled parts (target and distractor; reference and comparison).
2. Watch (5-10 s): a slow, schematic demo of one trial ending with the solution transformation (object rotates into alignment, paper unfolds, net folds, plane slices, camera walks to the viewpoint, MOT targets ringed and then moving). Arrows and outlines mark what matters; captions so it works muted; a replay button.
3. Try (3-5 easy practice trials, not logged): context hints appear the first time each element matters; input is never blocked; immediate feedback on each trial and the solution replay after errors.
4. Go: the first scored block starts easy and the staircase takes over. Later mechanics (mirror items, oblique cuts, more targets, faster speeds) get a one-time, one-line tip when they first appear.
5. "Skip" on every tutorial screen, "I've done this before" on first launch, "How to play" on every task screen; returning users skip by default.
6. Struggle help: after ~3 errors in a row or below 50% in a block, offer (never force) the demo and step the difficulty down.
7. No up-front text manual: at most two short sentences per screen.
8. Compare tutorial variants with opt-in, aggregate metrics; Andersen et al. found tutorials can lower engagement.
### (b) Gamification and session set
Include:
- Visible progress: an in-session bar (trial x of N, block dots) and a weekly-goal bar (goal gradient; competence).
- A self-chosen weekly goal (default 3 sessions of 10-15 min, any days) and a "days practised" calendar rather than a fragile daily chain (Lally; Lampit; Molloy).
- Honest levels: the real difficulty setting ("4 targets, speed 1.3") from adaptive staircases near 80-85% accuracy (a 3-down/1-up rule converges near 79% [unverified here]; Wilson et al.; Liu et al.).
- Learning curves per task (rolling median with a noise band, a dot per session) and personal bests computed over a full block, never a single trial; a label that early gains partly reflect learning the format (Uttal control groups g=0.45).
- Mastery achievements: a finite list with visible criteria, tied to skill or breadth (first 5-target track; 90% at level 8; tried every task; 12 practice days in a quarter); informational wording that names the level reached, never "mastered"; occasional unexpected informational notes ("1,000 rotations: here is your curve").
- An optional weekly streak (weeks meeting your own goal) with automatic grace and free repair by practising; it can be hidden; no daily streak by default.
- Sessions of 3-4 blocks of ~3 min with a break screen; users can stop after any block with progress saved; the session ends on its summary, a natural stopping point (inference).
- Reminders opt-in only: user-chosen time, at most one a day, never late at night, neutral wording ("Your session is ready if you want it"), auto-pause after 3 ignored (inference).
- Relatedness: opt-in share cards (task, level, personal change, the Chimera Discord link; no rank, no personal data); same-seed friend challenges compared only inside the group; cooperative community goals (total trials this month); community-made puzzle sets.
Avoid:
- Global or default leaderboards: comparison with others pulls attention to the self and discourages low performers (Kluger & DeNisi; Bai et al.; Hanus & Fox [unverified]). If the community wants one: opt-in, friends-only, per level.
- Loss framing and streak anxiety: "you'll lose your streak", countdowns, red badges, late-night guilt pushes, paid freezes; broken streaks demotivate most when self-blamed (Silverman & Barasch).
- Points, coins or login rewards for time on the app (engagement-contingent rewards: d=-0.40 on free choice); random loot; currencies.
- Fake progress (head-start stamps), "brain age", "IQ" scores, or population percentiles from self-selected users.
- Unlocking tasks by grinding (controlling): all task types open from day one; harder levels open by performance.
- Autoplay of the next session and "are you sure you want to quit?" prompts (ICO).
### (c) Feedback after errors and correct answers
- Every trial: correctness within ~0.5 s by icon (tick/cross) + word + colour, never colour alone; an optional short sound, off by default; no full-screen flashes.
- After an error (high-information feedback): show the right answer, then a short (~2-4 s, inference), slow, schematic replay of the transformation with signalling:
  - mental rotation: rotation-axis arrow while the comparison turns into alignment; for mirror pairs, best alignment and then the mirror flip;
  - paper folding: unfold step by step with holes propagating; cube nets: the net folds up with matching faces outlined;
  - cross-sections: the cutting plane sweeps through the solid and the section lifts out flat; perspective taking: the camera or figure moves to the target viewpoint;
  - MOT: freeze, then mark true targets and the user's picks with different shapes; optional slow replay of the last ~2 s with target trails.
- Controls: replay, pause/scrub, step, drag-to-rotate with button alternatives; "Continue" moves on; optional auto-advance in a speed mode.
- Show the most informative views first (Keehner et al.; James et al.); free manipulation is an extra.
- Wording is about the task ("The short arm ends up on the left"), never about the person, and never compares with others. Informational, not controlling: "Accuracy at level 6: 84% (last week 70%)", not "Don't stop now!".
- Fade: replays play automatically for the first few errors at each level, then only through a "Why?" button (expertise reversal).
- After a correct answer: brief confirmation and response time; "see solution" on slow correct answers; celebrate milestones (level up, personal best), not every trial.
- End of session: accuracy, median time, level change, comparison with your own history only, an honest line ("within your usual range" vs "a clear new best"), one suggested next step, then stop.
### (d) Safe claim wording
- Say: "Practise visuospatial puzzles: mental rotation, paper folding, cube nets, cross-sections, perspective taking and 3D object tracking." / "Your scores show how you do on these tasks in this app." / "Practice reliably improves the practised task and often similar spatial tests. It has not been shown to improve everyday life, school, work, sport, driving or IQ." / "Tasks are adapted from ones used in psychology research" (only if true, with links) / "Free, open source, no ads" (if true).
- Never say: "brain training that works", "boost your IQ" or "your brain", "scientifically/clinically proven", "improves focus, awareness, sport, driving or grades", "prevents decline or dementia", anything about a condition (ADHD, concussion, stroke), "brain age", "rewire your brain", percentiles against "the population"; no testimonials with undisclosed incentives.
- "May help" does not rescue an implied benefit (FTC 2022). Put the limitation sentence next to any results claim, not behind a link; disclose maintainers' ties to any research cited (Carrot case). Apply the same rule to community posts the project amplifies (inference).
### (e) Accessibility musts
- Flashes: target cues at most 3 flashes/s (better a steady ring or a pulse of 1 Hz or less), small area, no saturated-red flashing, no high-contrast stripes; check with a flash analyser such as PEAT [tool not reviewed].
- Motion: honour prefers-reduced-motion plus an in-app toggle for everything outside the stimulus (no camera moves, parallax or celebration animations; instant transitions); solution replays then start only on tap, or show 3-4 keyframes with arrows. Stimulus motion (MOT) is essential under WCAG 2.3.3, so warn before MOT and allow pause; log any slower or smaller comfort setting as its own condition.
- Colour: a colour-blind-safe palette plus shape or pattern for target, selected, correct and wrong; 3:1 contrast for objects and focus rings.
- Keyboard: every task playable by keys (F/J for same/mirror, number keys for options, Tab/arrows to cycle MOT objects with a visible focus ring, arrows to rotate); no held-key or rapid-press demands.
- Touch: targets of at least 44x44 CSS px (24 minimum); an invisible enlarged hit radius and nearest-object snapping for small moving balls; no drag-only controls.
- Time: untimed practice and adjustable response windows wherever speed is not the measure.
- Menus, settings and stats work with screen readers; charts have text summaries.
